    "Isotopes": "</div>\n<link rel=\"mw-deduplicated-inline-style\" href=\"mw-data:TemplateStyles:r1368532237\" /><div role=\"note\" class=\"hatnote navigation-not-searchable\">Main article: Isotopes of <a href=\"/school1/ptable/element/Li\" class=\"wiki-link\" title=\"Lithium (Li)\">lithium</a></div>\n<p>Naturally occurring <a href=\"/school1/ptable/element/Li\" class=\"wiki-link\" title=\"Lithium (Li)\">lithium</a> is composed of two stable isotopes, <sup>6</sup>Li and <sup>7</sup>Li, the latter being the more abundant (95.15% natural abundance). Both natural isotopes have anomalously low nuclear binding energy per nucleon (compared to the neighboring elements on the periodic table, <a href=\"/school1/ptable/element/He\" class=\"wiki-link\" title=\"Helium (He)\">helium</a> and <a href=\"/school1/ptable/element/Be\" class=\"wiki-link\" title=\"Beryllium (Be)\">beryllium</a>); <a href=\"/school1/ptable/element/Li\" class=\"wiki-link\" title=\"Lithium (Li)\">lithium</a> is the only low numbered element that can produce net energy through nuclear fission. The two lithium nuclei have lower binding energies per nucleon than any other stable nuclides other than <a href=\"/school1/ptable/element/H\" class=\"wiki-link\" title=\"Hydrogen (H)\">hydrogen</a>-1, deuterium and <a href=\"/school1/ptable/element/He\" class=\"wiki-link\" title=\"Helium (He)\">helium</a>-3. As a result of this, though very light in atomic weight, lithium is less common in the Solar System than 28 of the first 34 chemical elements. Seven radioisotopes have been characterized, the most stable being <sup>8</sup>Li with a half-life of 838 ms and <sup>9</sup>Li with a half-life of 178&#160;ms. All of the remaining radioactive isotopes have half-lives that are shorter than 8.6&#160;ms. The shortest-lived isotope is <sup>4</sup>Li, which decays through proton emission and has a half-life of 7.6 \u00d7 10<sup>\u221223</sup> s. The <sup>6</sup>Li isotope is one of only five stable nuclides to have both an odd number of protons and an odd number of neutrons, the other four stable odd-odd nuclides being <a href=\"/school1/ptable/element/H\" class=\"wiki-link\" title=\"Hydrogen (H)\">hydrogen</a>-2, <a href=\"/school1/ptable/element/B\" class=\"wiki-link\" title=\"Boron (B)\">boron</a>-10, <a href=\"/school1/ptable/element/N\" class=\"wiki-link\" title=\"Nitrogen (N)\">nitrogen</a>-14, and <a href=\"/school1/ptable/element/Ta\" class=\"wiki-link\" title=\"Tantalum (Ta)\">tantalum</a>-180m.\n</p><p>Lithium isotopes fractionate substantially during a wide variety of natural processes, including mineral formation (chemical precipitation), metabolism, and ion exchange. Lithium ions substitute for <a href=\"/school1/ptable/element/Mg\" class=\"wiki-link\" title=\"Magnesium (Mg)\">magnesium</a> and <a href=\"/school1/ptable/element/Fe\" class=\"wiki-link\" title=\"Iron (Fe)\">iron</a> in octahedral sites in clay minerals, where <sup>6</sup>Li is preferred to <sup>7</sup>Li, resulting in enrichment of the light isotope in processes of hyperfiltration and rock alteration. The exotic <sup>11</sup>Li is known to exhibit a neutron halo, with 2 neutrons orbiting around its nucleus of 3 protons and 6 neutrons. The process known as laser isotope separation can be used to separate lithium isotopes, in particular <sup>7</sup>Li from <sup>6</sup>Li.\n</p><p>Nuclear weapons manufacture and other nuclear physics applications are a major source of artificial lithium fractionation, with the light isotope <sup>6</sup>Li being retained by industry and military stockpiles to such an extent that it has caused slight but measurable change in the <sup>6</sup>Li to <sup>7</sup>Li ratios in natural sources, such as rivers. This has led to unusual uncertainty in the standardized atomic weight, since this quantity depends on the natural abundance ratios of these naturally occurring stable lithium isotopes, as they are available in commercial lithium mineral sources.\n</p><p>Both stable isotopes can be laser cooled and were used to produce the first quantum degenerate Bose\u2013Fermi mixture.\n</p>\n<div class=\"mw-heading mw-heading2\">",

Berzelius gave the alkaline material the name \"<i>lithion</i>/<i>lithina</i>\", from the Greek word <i>\u03bb\u03b9\u03b8o\u03c2</i> (transliterated as <i>lithos</i>, meaning \"stone\"), to reflect its discovery in a solid mineral, as opposed to <a href=\"/school1/ptable/element/K\" class=\"wiki-link\" title=\"Potassium (K)\">potassium</a>, which had been discovered in plant ashes, and <a href=\"/school1/ptable/element/Na\" class=\"wiki-link\" title=\"Sodium (Na)\">sodium</a>, which was known partly for its high abundance in animal blood. He named the new element \"<a href=\"/school1/ptable/element/Li\" class=\"wiki-link\" title=\"Lithium (Li)\">lithium</a>\".\n</p><p>Arfwedson later showed that this same element was present in the minerals spodumene and lepidolite. In 1818, Christian Gmelin was the first to observe that <a href=\"/school1/ptable/element/Li\" class=\"wiki-link\" title=\"Lithium (Li)\">lithium</a> salts give a bright red color to flame. However, both Arfwedson and Gmelin tried and failed to isolate the pure element from its salts. It was not isolated until 1821, when William Thomas Brande obtained it by electrolysis of lithium oxide, a process that had previously been employed by the chemist Sir Humphry Davy to isolate the alkali metals <a href=\"/school1/ptable/element/K\" class=\"wiki-link\" title=\"Potassium (K)\">potassium</a> and <a href=\"/school1/ptable/element/Na\" class=\"wiki-link\" title=\"Sodium (Na)\">sodium</a>.<sup class=\"noprint Inline-Template Template-Fact\" style=\"white-space:nowrap;\">&#91;<i><span title=\"This claim has too many footnotes for reading to be smooth. (August 2026)\">excessive citations</span></i>&#93;</sup> Brande also described some pure salts, such as the chloride, and, estimating that lithia (lithium oxide) contained about 55% metal, estimated its atomic weight to be around 9.8&#160;g/mol (modern value ~6.94 g/mol). In 1855, larger quantities were produced through the electrolysis of lithium chloride by Robert Bunsen and Augustus Matthiessen. The discovery of this procedure led to commercial production in 1923 by the German company Metallgesellschaft AG, which performed an electrolysis of a liquid mixture of lithium chloride and potassium chloride.\n</p><p>Australian psychiatrist John Cade is credited with reintroducing lithium to treat mania in 1949. Mogens Schou of Denmark continued Cade's research, starting in the 1950s. Throughout the mid-20th century, lithium's mood stabilizing applicability for mania and depression took off in Europe and the United States. As lithium cannot be patented, it was developed as a medicine by academia rather than drug companies.\n</p><p>Production and use underwent several drastic changes in history. The first major application was in high-temperature lithium greases for aircraft engines and similar applications in World War II and shortly after.
