<p><b>Einsteinium</b> is a synthetic chemical element; it has symbol <b>Es</b> and atomic number 99 and is a member of the actinide series and the seventh transuranium element. 
</p><p>Einsteinium was discovered as a component of the debris of the first <a href="/school1/ptable/element/H" class="wiki-link" title="Hydrogen (H)">hydrogen</a> bomb explosion in 1952. Its most common isotope, einsteinium-253 (<sup>253</sup>Es; half-life 20.47 days), is produced artificially from decay of <a href="/school1/ptable/element/Cf" class="wiki-link" title="Californium (Cf)">californium</a>-253 in a few dedicated high-power nuclear reactors with a total yield on the order of one milligram per year. The reactor synthesis is followed by a complex process of separating einsteinium-253 from other actinides and products of their decay. Other isotopes are synthesized in various laboratories, but in much smaller amounts, by bombarding heavy actinide elements with light ions. Due to the small amounts of einsteinium produced and the short half-life of its most common isotope, there are no practical applications for it except basic scientific research. In particular, einsteinium was used to synthesize, for the first time, 17 atoms of the new element <a href="/school1/ptable/element/Md" class="wiki-link" title="Mendelevium (Md)">mendelevium</a> in 1955.
</p><p>Einsteinium is a soft, silvery, paramagnetic metal. Its chemistry is typical of the late actinides, with a preponderance of the +3 oxidation state; the +2 oxidation state is also accessible, especially in solids. The high radioactivity of <sup>253</sup>Es produces a visible glow and rapidly damages its crystalline metal lattice, with released heat of about 1,000 watts per gram. Studying its properties is difficult due to <sup>253</sup>Es's decay to <a href="/school1/ptable/element/Bk" class="wiki-link" title="Berkelium (Bk)">berkelium</a>-249 and then californium-249 at a rate of about 3% per day. The longest-lived isotope of einsteinium, <sup>252</sup>Es (half-life 471.7 days) would be more suitable for investigation of physical properties, but it has proven far more difficult to produce and is available only in minute quantities, not in bulk. Einsteinium is the element with the highest atomic number which has been observed in macroscopic quantities in its pure form as einsteinium-253.
</p><p>Like all synthetic transuranium elements, isotopes of einsteinium are very radioactive and are considered highly dangerous to health on ingestion.
</p>

## History
<figure class="mw-default-size mw-halign-left" typeof="mw:File/Thumb"><img src="/school1/ptable/theme-assets/default/images/inline/956529233fd4f0c84cc1369cbe526e87.jpg" decoding="async" width="250" height="199" class="mw-file-element" srcset="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/5/58/Ivy_Mike_-_mushroom_cloud.jpg/500px-Ivy_Mike_-_mushroom_cloud.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&amp;utm_campaign=parser&amp;utm_content=thumbnail 2x" data-file-width="800" data-file-height="637" /><figcaption><a href="/school1/ptable/element/Es" class="wiki-link" title="Einsteinium (Es)">Einsteinium</a> was first observed in the fallout from the <i>Ivy Mike</i> nuclear test.</figcaption></figure>
<p><a href="/school1/ptable/element/Es" class="wiki-link" title="Einsteinium (Es)">Einsteinium</a> was first identified in December 1952 by Albert Ghiorso and co-workers at University of California, Berkeley in collaboration with the Argonne and Los Alamos National Laboratories, in the fallout from the <i>Ivy Mike</i> nuclear test. The test was done on November 1, 1952, at Enewetak Atoll in the Pacific Ocean and was the first successful test of a thermonuclear weapon. Initial examination of the debris from the explosion had shown the production of a new isotope of <a href="/school1/ptable/element/Pu" class="wiki-link" title="Plutonium (Pu)">plutonium</a>, <sup>244</sup>Pu, which could only have formed by the absorption of six neutrons by a <a href="/school1/ptable/element/U" class="wiki-link" title="Uranium (U)">uranium</a>-238 nucleus followed by two beta decays.

<p>At the time, the multiple neutron absorption was thought to be an extremely rare process, but the identification of <sup>244</sup>Pu indicated that still more neutrons could have been captured by the <a href="/school1/ptable/element/U" class="wiki-link" title="Uranium (U)">uranium</a>, producing new elements heavier than <a href="/school1/ptable/element/Cf" class="wiki-link" title="Californium (Cf)">californium</a>.
</p>
<figure class="mw-default-size mw-halign-left" typeof="mw:File/Thumb"><img src="/school1/ptable/theme-assets/default/images/inline/0945ec957aedca341d81c6c6ddaff4e6.jpg" decoding="async" width="190" height="249" style="--mw-file-upright: 0.75" class="mw-file-element mw-file-upright" srcset="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/c/cc/Albert_Ghiorso_ca_1970.jpg/500px-Albert_Ghiorso_ca_1970.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&amp;utm_campaign=parser&amp;utm_content=thumbnail 2x" data-file-width="781" data-file-height="1024" /><figcaption>The element was discovered by a team headed by Albert Ghiorso.</figcaption></figure>
<p>Ghiorso and co-workers analyzed filter papers which had been flown through the explosion cloud on airplanes (the same sampling technique that had been used to discover <sup>244</sup>Pu). Larger amounts of radioactive material were later isolated from coral debris of the atoll, and these were delivered to the U.S. The separation of suspected new elements was carried out in the presence of a citric acid/ammonium buffer solution in a weakly acidic medium (pH ≈ 3.5), using ion exchange at elevated temperatures; fewer than 200 atoms of <a href="/school1/ptable/element/Es" class="wiki-link" title="Einsteinium (Es)">einsteinium</a> were recovered in the end. Nevertheless, element 99, einsteinium, and in particular <sup>253</sup>Es, could be detected via its characteristic high-energy alpha decay at 6.6&#160;MeV. It was produced by the capture of 15 neutrons by <a href="/school1/ptable/element/U" class="wiki-link" title="Uranium (U)">uranium</a>-238 nuclei followed by seven beta decays, and had a half-life of 20.5 days. Such multiple neutron absorption was made possible by the high neutron flux density during the detonation, so that newly generated heavy isotopes had plenty of available neutrons to absorb before they could disintegrate into lighter elements. <p>Some <sup>238</sup>U atoms, however, could absorb two additional neutrons (for a total of 17), resulting in <sup>255</sup>Es, as well as in the <sup>255</sup>Fm isotope of another new element, <a href="/school1/ptable/element/Fm" class="wiki-link" title="Fermium (Fm)">fermium</a>. The discovery of the new elements and the associated new data on multiple neutron capture were initially kept secret on the orders of the U.S. military until 1955 due to Cold War tensions and competition with Soviet Union in nuclear technologies. However, the rapid capture of so many neutrons would provide needed direct experimental confirmation  of the r-process multi-neutron absorption needed to explain the cosmic nucleosynthesis (production) of certain heavy elements (heavier than <a href="/school1/ptable/element/Ni" class="wiki-link" title="Nickel (Ni)">nickel</a>) in supernovas, before beta decay. Such a process is needed to explain the existence of many stable elements in the universe.
</p><p>Meanwhile, isotopes of element 99 (as well as of new element 100, <a href="/school1/ptable/element/Fm" class="wiki-link" title="Fermium (Fm)">fermium</a>) were produced in the Berkeley and Argonne laboratories, in a nuclear reaction between <a href="/school1/ptable/element/N" class="wiki-link" title="Nitrogen (N)">nitrogen</a>-14 and uranium-238, and later by intense neutron irradiation of <a href="/school1/ptable/element/Pu" class="wiki-link" title="Plutonium (Pu)">plutonium</a> or <a href="/school1/ptable/element/Cf" class="wiki-link" title="Californium (Cf)">californium</a>:

<p>These results were published in several articles in 1954 with the disclaimer that these were not the first studies that had been carried out on the elements. The Berkeley team also reported some results on the chemical properties of einsteinium and <a href="/school1/ptable/element/Fm" class="wiki-link" title="Fermium (Fm)">fermium</a>. The <i>Ivy Mike</i> results were declassified and published in 1955.

<p>In their discovery of elements 99 and 100, the American teams had competed with a group at the Nobel Institute for Physics, Stockholm, Sweden. In late 1953 to early 1954, the Swedish group succeeded in synthesizing light isotopes of element 100, in particular <sup>250</sup>Fm, by bombarding uranium with <a href="/school1/ptable/element/O" class="wiki-link" title="Oxygen (O)">oxygen</a> nuclei. These results were also published in 1954. Nevertheless, the priority of the Berkeley team was generally recognized, as its publications preceded the Swedish article, and they were based on the previously undisclosed results of the 1952 thermonuclear explosion; thus the Berkeley team was given the privilege to name the new elements. As the effort which had led to the design of <i>Ivy Mike</i> was codenamed Project PANDA, element 99 had been jokingly nicknamed "Pandemonium" but the official names suggested by the Berkeley group derived from two prominent scientists, Einstein and Fermi: "We suggest for the name for the element with the atomic number 99, einsteinium (symbol E) after Albert Einstein and for the name for the element with atomic number 100, fermium (symbol Fm), after Enrico Fermi." Both Einstein and Fermi died between the time the names were originally proposed and when they were announced. The discovery of these new elements was announced by Albert Ghiorso at the first Geneva Atomic Conference held on 8–20 August 1955. The symbol for einsteinium was first given as "E" and later changed to "Es" by IUPAC.
</p>

## Isotopes
<style data-mw-deduplicate="TemplateStyles:r1368532237">.mw-parser-output .hatnote{font-style:italic}.mw-parser-output div.hatnote{padding-left:1.6em;margin-bottom:0.5em}.mw-parser-output .hatnote i{font-style:normal}.mw-parser-output .hatnote+.mw-empty-elt+.hatnote{margin-top:-0.5em}@media print{body.ns-0 .mw-parser-output .hatnote{display:none!important}}</style><div role="note" class="hatnote navigation-not-searchable">Main article: Isotopes of <a href="/school1/ptable/element/Es" class="wiki-link" title="Einsteinium (Es)">einsteinium</a></div>
<p>Eighteen isotopes and four nuclear isomers are known for <a href="/school1/ptable/element/Es" class="wiki-link" title="Einsteinium (Es)">einsteinium</a>, with mass numbers 240–257. All are radioactive; the most stable one, <sup>252</sup>Es, has half-life 471.7 days. The next most stable isotopes are <sup>254</sup>Es (half-life 275.7 days), <sup>255</sup>Es (39.8 days), and <sup>253</sup>Es (20.47 days). All the other isotopes have half-lives shorter than 40 hours, most shorter than 30 minutes. Of the five isomers, the most stable is <sup>254m</sup>Es with a half-life of 39.3 hours.
</p>
<div class="mw-heading mw-heading3">

## Applications
<p>There is almost no use for any isotope of <a href="/school1/ptable/element/Es" class="wiki-link" title="Einsteinium (Es)">einsteinium</a> outside basic scientific research aiming at production of higher transuranium elements and superheavy elements.
</p><p>In 1955, <a href="/school1/ptable/element/Md" class="wiki-link" title="Mendelevium (Md)">mendelevium</a> was synthesized by irradiating a target consisting of about 10<sup>9</sup> atoms of <sup>253</sup>Es in the 60-inch cyclotron at Berkeley Laboratory. The resulting <sup>253</sup>Es(α,n)<sup>256</sup>Md reaction yielded 17 atoms of the new element with the atomic number of 101.
</p><p>The rare isotope <sup>254</sup>Es is favored for production of superheavy elements due to its large mass, relatively long half-life of 270 days, and availability in significant amounts of several micrograms. Hence <sup>254</sup>Es was used as a target in the attempted synthesis of ununennium (element 119) in 1985 by bombarding it with <a href="/school1/ptable/element/Ca" class="wiki-link" title="Calcium (Ca)">calcium</a>-48 ions at the superHILAC linear particle accelerator at Berkeley, California. No atoms were identified, setting an upper limit for the cross section of this reaction at 300 nanobarns.
</p>
