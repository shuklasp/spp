    "History": "</div>\n<p>Molybdenite—the principal ore from which <a href=\"/school1/ptable/element/Mo\" class=\"wiki-link\" title=\"Molybdenum (Mo)\">molybdenum</a> is now extracted—was previously known as molybdena. Molybdena was confused with and often used as though it were graphite. Like graphite, molybdenite can be used to blacken a surface or as a solid lubricant. Even when molybdena was distinguishable from graphite, it was still confused with the common <a href=\"/school1/ptable/element/Pb\" class=\"wiki-link\" title=\"Lead (Pb)\">lead</a> ore PbS (now called galena); the name comes from Ancient Greek <span title=\"Ancient Greek (to 1453)-language text\"><span lang=\"grc\">μόλυβδος</span></span> <span title=\"Ancient Greek (to 1453)-language text\"><i lang=\"grc-Latn\">mólybdos</i></span>, meaning <i><a href=\"/school1/ptable/element/Pb\" class=\"wiki-link\" title=\"Lead (Pb)\">lead</a></i>. (The Greek word itself has been proposed as a loanword from Anatolian Luvian and Lydian languages).\n</p><p>Although (reportedly) <a href=\"/school1/ptable/element/Mo\" class=\"wiki-link\" title=\"Molybdenum (Mo)\">molybdenum</a> was deliberately alloyed with steel in one 14th-century Japanese sword (mfd. <abbr title=\"circa\">c.</abbr><span style=\"white-space:nowrap;\">&#8201;1330</span>), that art was never employed widely and was later lost. In the West in 1754, Bengt Andersson Qvist examined a sample of molybdenite and determined that it did not contain <a href=\"/school1/ptable/element/Pb\" class=\"wiki-link\" title=\"Lead (Pb)\">lead</a> and thus was not galena.\n</p><p>By 1778 Swedish chemist Carl Wilhelm Scheele stated firmly that molybdena was (indeed) neither galena nor graphite. Instead, Scheele correctly proposed that molybdena was an ore of a distinct new element, and from which it might be isolated. Peter Jacob Hjelm successfully isolated a metal he called <span title=\"Latin-language text\"><i lang=\"la\">molybdaenum</i></span> using <a href=\"/school1/ptable/element/C\" class=\"wiki-link\" title=\"Carbon (C)\">carbon</a> and linseed oil in 1781.<sup class=\"reference nowrap\"><span title=\"Page / location: 275\">&#58;&#8202;275&#8202;</span></sup>\n</p><p>For the next century, <a href=\"/school1/ptable/element/Mo\" class=\"wiki-link\" title=\"Molybdenum (Mo)\">molybdenum</a> had no industrial use. It was relatively scarce, the pure metal was difficult to extract, and the necessary techniques of metallurgy were immature. Early molybdenum steel alloys showed great promise of increased hardness, but efforts to manufacture the alloys on a large scale were hampered with inconsistent results, a tendency toward brittleness, and recrystallization. In 1906, William D. Coolidge filed a patent for rendering molybdenum ductile, leading to applications as a heating element for high-temperature furnaces and as a support for <a href=\"/school1/ptable/element/W\" class=\"wiki-link\" title=\"Tungsten (W)\">tungsten</a>-filament light bulbs; oxide formation and degradation require that molybdenum be physically sealed or held in an inert gas. In 1913, Frank E. Elmore developed a froth flotation process to recover molybdenite from ores; flotation remains the primary isolation process.\n</p><p>During World War I, demand for molybdenum spiked; it was used both in armor plating and as a substitute for <a href=\"/school1/ptable/element/W\" class=\"wiki-link\" title=\"Tungsten (W)\">tungsten</a> in high-speed steels. Some British tanks were protected by 75&#160;mm (3&#160;in) <a href=\"/school1/ptable/element/Mn\" class=\"wiki-link\" title=\"Manganese (Mn)\">manganese</a> steel plating, but this proved to be ineffective. The <a href=\"/school1/ptable/element/Mn\" class=\"wiki-link\" title=\"Manganese (Mn)\">manganese</a> steel plates were replaced with much lighter 25&#160;mm (1.0&#160;in) molybdenum steel plates allowing for higher speed, greater maneuverability, and better protection. The Germans also used molybdenum-doped steel for heavy artillery, like in the super-heavy howitzer Big Bertha, because traditional steel melts at the temperatures produced by the propellant of the one ton shell. After the war, demand plummeted until metallurgical advances allowed extensive development of peacetime applications. In World War II, molybdenum again saw strategic importance as a substitute for <a href=\"/school1/ptable/element/W\" class=\"wiki-link\" title=\"Tungsten (W)\">tungsten</a> in steel alloys.\n</p>\n<div class=\"mw-heading mw-heading2\">",
