Characteristics: <p><a href="/school1/ptable/element/Pd" class="wiki-link" title="Palladium (Pd)">Palladium</a> belongs to group&#160;10 in the periodic table, but the configuration in the outermost electrons is in accordance with Hund's rule. <p>This 5s<sup>0</sup> configuration, unique in period&#160;5, makes <a href="/school1/ptable/element/Pd" class="wiki-link" title="Palladium (Pd)">palladium</a> the heaviest element having only <i>one</i> incomplete electron shell, with all shells above it empty.
</p><p>Palladium has the appearance of a soft <a href="/school1/ptable/element/Ag" class="wiki-link" title="Silver (Ag)">silver</a>-white metal that resembles <a href="/school1/ptable/element/Pt" class="wiki-link" title="Platinum (Pt)">platinum</a>. It is the least dense and has the lowest melting point of the <a href="/school1/ptable/element/Pt" class="wiki-link" title="Platinum (Pt)">platinum</a> group metals. It is soft and ductile when annealed and is greatly increased in strength and hardness when cold-worked. Palladium dissolves slowly in concentrated nitric acid, in hot, concentrated sulfuric acid, and when finely ground, in hydrochloric acid. It dissolves readily at room temperature in aqua regia.
</p><p>Palladium does not react with <a href="/school1/ptable/element/O" class="wiki-link" title="Oxygen (O)">oxygen</a> at standard temperature (and thus does not tarnish in air). Palladium heated to 800&#160;°C will produce a layer of palladium(II) oxide (PdO). It may slowly develop a slight brownish coloration over time, likely due to the formation of a surface layer of its monoxide.
</p><p>Palladium films with defects produced by alpha particle bombardment at low temperature exhibit superconductivity having <i>T</i><sub>c</sub> = 3.2 K.
</p>
<div class="mw-heading mw-heading3">
Isotopes: <link rel="mw-deduplicated-inline-style" href="mw-data:TemplateStyles:r1368532237" /><div role="note" class="hatnote navigation-not-searchable">Main article: Isotopes of <a href="/school1/ptable/element/Pd" class="wiki-link" title="Palladium (Pd)">palladium</a></div>
<p>Naturally occurring <a href="/school1/ptable/element/Pd" class="wiki-link" title="Palladium (Pd)">palladium</a> is composed of six stable isotopes. The most stable radioisotopes are <sup>107</sup>Pd with a half-life of 6.5 million years (traces found in nature), <sup>103</sup>Pd with a half-life of 16.99 days, and <sup>100</sup>Pd with a half-life of 3.63 days. There are 25 other radioisotopes characterized ranging from <sup>91</sup>Pd to <sup>129</sup>Pd. These have half-lives of less than thirty minutes, except <sup>101</sup>Pd (8.47 hours), <sup>109</sup>Pd (13.6 hours), and <sup>112</sup>Pd (21.0 hours).
</p><p>For isotopes with atomic masses less than that of the most abundant stable isotope, <sup>106</sup>Pd, the primary decay mode is electron capture with the primary decay product being <a href="/school1/ptable/element/Rh" class="wiki-link" title="Rhodium (Rh)">rhodium</a>. The primary mode of decay for those isotopes of Pd with atomic mass greater than 106 is beta decay with the primary product of this decay being <a href="/school1/ptable/element/Ag" class="wiki-link" title="Silver (Ag)">silver</a>.
</p><p>Radiogenic <sup>107</sup>Ag is a decay product of <sup>107</sup>Pd and was first discovered in 1978 in the Santa Clara meteorite of 1976. The discoverers suggest that the coalescence and differentiation of <a href="/school1/ptable/element/Fe" class="wiki-link" title="Iron (Fe)">iron</a>-cored small planets may have occurred 10 million years after a nucleosynthetic event. <sup>107</sup>Pd versus Ag correlations observed in Solar System bodies must reflect the presence of short-lived nuclides in the early Solar System.
</p><p><span class="chemf nowrap"><span class="nowrap"><span style="display:inline-block;margin-bottom:-0.3em;vertical-align:0.5em;line-height:1em;font-size:80%;text-align:left"><sup style="font-size:inherit;line-height:inherit;vertical-align:baseline">107</sup><br /><sub style="font-size:inherit;line-height:inherit;vertical-align:baseline"></sub></span></span>Pd</span> is also produced as a fission product in spontaneous or induced fission of <span class="chemf nowrap"><span class="nowrap"><span style="display:inline-block;margin-bottom:-0.3em;vertical-align:0.5em;line-height:1em;font-size:80%;text-align:left"><sup style="font-size:inherit;line-height:inherit;vertical-align:baseline">235</sup><br /><sub style="font-size:inherit;line-height:inherit;vertical-align:baseline"></sub></span></span>U</span>. As it is not very mobile in the environment and has a relatively low decay energy, <span class="chemf nowrap"><span class="nowrap"><span style="display:inline-block;margin-bottom:-0.3em;vertical-align:0.5em;line-height:1em;font-size:80%;text-align:left"><sup style="font-size:inherit;line-height:inherit;vertical-align:baseline">107</sup><br /><sub style="font-size:inherit;line-height:inherit;vertical-align:baseline"></sub></span></span>Pd</span> is usually considered to be among the more benign of the long-lived fission products.
</p>

Occurrence: <figure class="mw-default-size" typeof="mw:File/Thumb"><img src="/school1/ptable/theme-assets/default/images/inline/50ca7d62cbfc880192921a931f7bf5e7.PNG" decoding="async" width="400" height="175" style="--mw-file-upright: 1.6" class="mw-file-element mw-file-upright" srcset="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/1/13/2005palladium_%28mined%29.PNG/960px-2005palladium_%28mined%29.PNG?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&amp;utm_campaign=parser&amp;utm_content=thumbnail 2x" data-file-width="1425" data-file-height="625" /><figcaption><a href="/school1/ptable/element/Pd" class="wiki-link" title="Palladium (Pd)">Palladium</a> output in 2005</figcaption></figure>
<p>As overall mine production of <a href="/school1/ptable/element/Pd" class="wiki-link" title="Palladium (Pd)">palladium</a> reached 210,000 kilograms in 2022, Russia was the top producer with 88,000 kilograms, followed by South Africa, Canada, the U.S., and Zimbabwe. Russia's company Norilsk <a href="/school1/ptable/element/Ni" class="wiki-link" title="Nickel (Ni)">Nickel</a> ranks first among the largest <a href="/school1/ptable/element/Pd" class="wiki-link" title="Palladium (Pd)">palladium</a> producers globally, accounting for 39% of the world's production. 
</p><p>Palladium can be found as a free metal alloyed with <a href="/school1/ptable/element/Au" class="wiki-link" title="Gold (Au)">gold</a> and other <a href="/school1/ptable/element/Pt" class="wiki-link" title="Platinum (Pt)">platinum</a>-group metals in placer deposits of the Ural Mountains, Australia, Ethiopia, North and South America. For the production of palladium, these deposits play only a minor role. The most important commercial sources are <a href="/school1/ptable/element/Ni" class="wiki-link" title="Nickel (Ni)">nickel</a>-<a href="/school1/ptable/element/Cu" class="wiki-link" title="Copper (Cu)">copper</a> deposits found in the Sudbury Basin, Ontario, and the Norilsk–Talnakh deposits in Siberia. The other large deposit is the Merensky Reef <a href="/school1/ptable/element/Pt" class="wiki-link" title="Platinum (Pt)">platinum</a> group metals deposit within the Bushveld Igneous Complex South Africa. The Stillwater igneous complex of Montana and the Roby zone ore body of the Lac des Îles igneous complex of Ontario are the two other sources of palladium in Canada and the United States. Palladium is found in the rare minerals cooperite and polarite. Many more Pd minerals are known, but all of them are very rare.
</p><p>Palladium is also produced in nuclear fission reactors and can be extracted from spent nuclear fuel (see synthesis of precious metals), though this source for palladium is not used. None of the existing nuclear reprocessing facilities are equipped to extract palladium from the high-level radioactive waste. A complication for the recovery of palladium in spent fuel is the presence of <span class="chemf nowrap"><span class="nowrap"><span style="display:inline-block;margin-bottom:-0.3em;vertical-align:0.5em;line-height:1em;font-size:80%;text-align:left"><sup style="font-size:inherit;line-height:inherit;vertical-align:baseline">107</sup><br /><sub style="font-size:inherit;line-height:inherit;vertical-align:baseline"></sub></span></span>Pd</span>, a slightly radioactive long-lived fission product. Depending on end use, the radioactivity contributed by the <span class="chemf nowrap"><span class="nowrap"><span style="display:inline-block;margin-bottom:-0.3em;vertical-align:0.5em;line-height:1em;font-size:80%;text-align:left"><sup style="font-size:inherit;line-height:inherit;vertical-align:baseline">107</sup><br /><sub style="font-size:inherit;line-height:inherit;vertical-align:baseline"></sub></span></span>Pd</span> might make the recovered palladium unusable without a costly step of isotope separation.
</p>
