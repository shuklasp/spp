=== Isotopes ===
</div>
<link rel="mw-deduplicated-inline-style" href="mw-data:TemplateStyles:r1368532237" /><div role="note" class="hatnote navigation-not-searchable">Main article: Isotopes of <a href="/school1/ptable/element/Ni" class="wiki-link" title="Nickel (Ni)">nickel</a></div>
<p>The isotopes of <a href="/school1/ptable/element/Ni" class="wiki-link" title="Nickel (Ni)">nickel</a> range in atomic mass from <span class="nowrap"><span data-sort-value="7001480000000000000♠"></span>48&#160;Da</span> (<span class="chemf nowrap"><span class="nowrap"><span style="display:inline-block;margin-bottom:-0.3em;vertical-align:0.5em;line-height:1em;font-size:80%;text-align:left"><sup style="font-size:inherit;line-height:inherit;vertical-align:baseline">48</sup><br /><sub style="font-size:inherit;line-height:inherit;vertical-align:baseline"></sub></span></span>Ni</span>) to <span class="nowrap"><span data-sort-value="7001820000000000000♠"></span>82&#160;Da</span> (<span class="chemf nowrap"><span class="nowrap"><span style="display:inline-block;margin-bottom:-0.3em;vertical-align:0.5em;line-height:1em;font-size:80%;text-align:left"><sup style="font-size:inherit;line-height:inherit;vertical-align:baseline">82</sup><br /><sub style="font-size:inherit;line-height:inherit;vertical-align:baseline"></sub></span></span>Ni</span>).
</p><p>Natural <a href="/school1/ptable/element/Ni" class="wiki-link" title="Nickel (Ni)">nickel</a> is composed of five stable isotopes, <span class="chemf nowrap"><span class="nowrap"><span style="display:inline-block;margin-bottom:-0.3em;vertical-align:0.5em;line-height:1em;font-size:80%;text-align:left"><sup style="font-size:inherit;line-height:inherit;vertical-align:baseline">58</sup><br /><sub style="font-size:inherit;line-height:inherit;vertical-align:baseline"></sub></span></span>Ni</span>, <span class="chemf nowrap"><span class="nowrap"><span style="display:inline-block;margin-bottom:-0.3em;vertical-align:0.5em;line-height:1em;font-size:80%;text-align:left"><sup style="font-size:inherit;line-height:inherit;vertical-align:baseline">60</sup><br /><sub style="font-size:inherit;line-height:inherit;vertical-align:baseline"></sub></span></span>Ni</span>, <span class="chemf nowrap"><span class="nowrap"><span style="display:inline-block;margin-bottom:-0.3em;vertical-align:0.5em;line-height:1em;font-size:80%;text-align:left"><sup style="font-size:inherit;line-height:inherit;vertical-align:baseline">61</sup><br /><sub style="font-size:inherit;line-height:inherit;vertical-align:baseline"></sub></span></span>Ni</span>, <span class="chemf nowrap"><span class="nowrap"><span style="display:inline-block;margin-bottom:-0.3em;vertical-align:0.5em;line-height:1em;font-size:80%;text-align:left"><sup style="font-size:inherit;line-height:inherit;vertical-align:baseline">62</sup><br /><sub style="font-size:inherit;line-height:inherit;vertical-align:baseline"></sub></span></span>Ni</span> and <span class="chemf nowrap"><span class="nowrap"><span style="display:inline-block;margin-bottom:-0.3em;vertical-align:0.5em;line-height:1em;font-size:80%;text-align:left"><sup style="font-size:inherit;line-height:inherit;vertical-align:baseline">64</sup><br /><sub style="font-size:inherit;line-height:inherit;vertical-align:baseline"></sub></span></span>Ni</span>, of which <span class="chemf nowrap"><span class="nowrap"><span style="display:inline-block;margin-bottom:-0.3em;vertical-align:0.5em;line-height:1em;font-size:80%;text-align:left"><sup style="font-size:inherit;line-height:inherit;vertical-align:baseline">58</sup><br /><sub style="font-size:inherit;line-height:inherit;vertical-align:baseline"></sub></span></span>Ni</span> is the most abundant (68.077% natural abundance).
</p><p>Nickel-62 has the highest binding energy per nucleon of any nuclide: 8.7946&#160;MeV/nucleon. Its binding energy is greater than both <span class="chemf nowrap"><span class="nowrap"><span style="display:inline-block;margin-bottom:-0.3em;vertical-align:0.5em;line-height:1em;font-size:80%;text-align:left"><sup style="font-size:inherit;line-height:inherit;vertical-align:baseline">56</sup><br /><sub style="font-size:inherit;line-height:inherit;vertical-align:baseline"></sub></span></span>Fe</span> and <span class="chemf nowrap"><span class="nowrap"><span style="display:inline-block;margin-bottom:-0.3em;vertical-align:0.5em;line-height:1em;font-size:80%;text-align:left"><sup style="font-size:inherit;line-height:inherit;vertical-align:baseline">58</sup><br /><sub style="font-size:inherit;line-height:inherit;vertical-align:baseline"></sub></span></span>Fe</span>, more abundant nuclides often incorrectly cited as having the highest binding energy. Though this would seem to predict nickel as the most abundant heavy element in the universe, the high rate of photodisintegration of nickel in stellar interiors causes <a href="/school1/ptable/element/Fe" class="wiki-link" title="Iron (Fe)">iron</a> to be by far the most abundant.
</p><p>Nickel-60 is the daughter product of the extinct radionuclide <span class="chemf nowrap"><span class="nowrap"><span style="display:inline-block;margin-bottom:-0.3em;vertical-align:0.5em;line-height:1em;font-size:80%;text-align:left"><sup style="font-size:inherit;line-height:inherit;vertical-align:baseline">60</sup><br /><sub style="font-size:inherit;line-height:inherit;vertical-align:baseline"></sub></span></span>Fe</span> (half-life 2.6&#160;million years). Due to the long half-life of <span class="chemf nowrap"><span class="nowrap"><span style="display:inline-block;margin-bottom:-0.3em;vertical-align:0.5em;line-height:1em;font-size:80%;text-align:left"><sup style="font-size:inherit;line-height:inherit;vertical-align:baseline">60</sup><br /><sub style="font-size:inherit;line-height:inherit;vertical-align:baseline"></sub></span></span>Fe</span>, its persistence in materials in the Solar System may generate observable variations in the isotopic composition of <span class="chemf nowrap"><span class="nowrap"><span style="display:inline-block;margin-bottom:-0.3em;vertical-align:0.5em;line-height:1em;font-size:80%;text-align:left"><sup style="font-size:inherit;line-height:inherit;vertical-align:baseline">60</sup><br /><sub style="font-size:inherit;line-height:inherit;vertical-align:baseline"></sub></span></span>Ni</span>. Therefore, the abundance of <span class="chemf nowrap"><span class="nowrap"><span style="display:inline-block;margin-bottom:-0.3em;vertical-align:0.5em;line-height:1em;font-size:80%;text-align:left"><sup style="font-size:inherit;line-height:inherit;vertical-align:baseline">60</sup><br /><sub style="font-size:inherit;line-height:inherit;vertical-align:baseline"></sub></span></span>Ni</span> in extraterrestrial material may give insight into the origin of the Solar System and its early history.
</p><p>At least 26 nickel radioisotopes have been characterized; the most stable are <span class="chemf nowrap"><span class="nowrap"><span style="display:inline-block;margin-bottom:-0.3em;vertical-align:0.5em;line-height:1em;font-size:80%;text-align:left"><sup style="font-size:inherit;line-height:inherit;vertical-align:baseline">59</sup><br /><sub style="font-size:inherit;line-height:inherit;vertical-align:baseline"></sub></span></span>Ni</span> with half-life 76,000 years, <span class="chemf nowrap"><span class="nowrap"><span style="display:inline-block;margin-bottom:-0.3em;vertical-align:0.5em;line-height:1em;font-size:80%;text-align:left"><sup style="font-size:inherit;line-height:inherit;vertical-align:baseline">63</sup><br /><sub style="font-size:inherit;line-height:inherit;vertical-align:baseline"></sub></span></span>Ni</span> (100 years), and <span class="chemf nowrap"><span class="nowrap"><span style="display:inline-block;margin-bottom:-0.3em;vertical-align:0.5em;line-height:1em;font-size:80%;text-align:left"><sup style="font-size:inherit;line-height:inherit;vertical-align:baseline">56</sup><br /><sub style="font-size:inherit;line-height:inherit;vertical-align:baseline"></sub></span></span>Ni</span> (6 days). All other radioisotopes have half-lives less than 60 hours and most these have half-lives less than 30 seconds. This element also has one meta state.
</p><p>Radioactive nickel-56 is produced by the <a href="/school1/ptable/element/Si" class="wiki-link" title="Silicon (Si)">silicon</a> burning process and later set free in large amounts in Type Ia supernovae. The shape of the light curve of these supernovae at intermediate to late-times corresponds to the decay via electron capture of <span class="chemf nowrap"><span class="nowrap"><span style="display:inline-block;margin-bottom:-0.3em;vertical-align:0.5em;line-height:1em;font-size:80%;text-align:left"><sup style="font-size:inherit;line-height:inherit;vertical-align:baseline">56</sup><br /><sub style="font-size:inherit;line-height:inherit;vertical-align:baseline"></sub></span></span>Ni</span> to <a href="/school1/ptable/element/Co" class="wiki-link" title="Cobalt (Co)">cobalt</a>-56 and ultimately to <a href="/school1/ptable/element/Fe" class="wiki-link" title="Iron (Fe)">iron</a>-56. Nickel-59 is a long-lived cosmogenic radionuclide; half-life 76,000 years. <span class="chemf nowrap"><span class="nowrap"><span style="display:inline-block;margin-bottom:-0.3em;vertical-align:0.5em;line-height:1em;font-size:80%;text-align:left"><sup style="font-size:inherit;line-height:inherit;vertical-align:baseline">59</sup><br /><sub style="font-size:inherit;line-height:inherit;vertical-align:baseline"></sub></span></span>Ni</span> has found many applications in isotope geology. <span class="chemf nowrap"><span class="nowrap"><span style="display:inline-block;margin-bottom:-0.3em;vertical-align:0.5em;line-height:1em;font-size:80%;text-align:left"><sup style="font-size:inherit;line-height:inherit;vertical-align:baseline">59</sup><br /><sub style="font-size:inherit;line-height:inherit;vertical-align:baseline"></sub></span></span>Ni</span> has been used to date the terrestrial age of meteorites and to determine abundances of extraterrestrial dust in ice and sediment. Nickel-78, with a half-life of 110 milliseconds, is believed an important isotope in supernova nucleosynthesis of elements heavier than <a href="/school1/ptable/element/Fe" class="wiki-link" title="Iron (Fe)">iron</a>. <sup>48</sup>Ni, discovered in 1999, is the most proton-rich heavy element isotope known. With 28 protons and 20 neutrons, <sup>48</sup>Ni is "doubly magic", as is <sup>78</sup>Ni with 28 protons and 50 neutrons. Both are therefore unusually stable for nuclei with so large a proton–neutron imbalance.
</p><p>Nickel-63 is a contaminant found in the support structure of nuclear reactors. It is produced through neutron capture by nickel-62. Small amounts have also been found near nuclear weapon test sites in the South Pacific.
</p>

=== Occurrence ===
</div>
<link rel="mw-deduplicated-inline-style" href="mw-data:TemplateStyles:r1368532237" /><div role="note" class="hatnote navigation-not-searchable">See also: Ore genesis and Category:<a href="/school1/ptable/element/Ni" class="wiki-link" title="Nickel (Ni)">Nickel</a> minerals</div>
<figure class="mw-default-size mw-halign-left" typeof="mw:File/Thumb"><img src="/school1/ptable/theme-assets/default/images/inline/6ffc41fc5bfe79dfd983b271ad437f91.jpg" decoding="async" width="250" height="188" class="mw-file-element"  data-file-width="3264" data-file-height="2448" /><figcaption>Widmanstätten pattern showing the two forms of <a href="/school1/ptable/element/Ni" class="wiki-link" title="Nickel (Ni)">nickel</a>–<a href="/school1/ptable/element/Fe" class="wiki-link" title="Iron (Fe)">iron</a>, kamacite and taenite, in an octahedrite meteorite</figcaption></figure>
<p><a href="/school1/ptable/element/Ni" class="wiki-link" title="Nickel (Ni)">Nickel</a> ores are classified as oxides or sulfides.  Oxides include laterite, where the principal mineral mixtures are nickeliferous limonite, (Fe,Ni)O(OH), and garnierite (a mixture of various hydrous nickel and nickel-rich silicates). Nickel sulfides commonly exist as solid solutions with <a href="/school1/ptable/element/Fe" class="wiki-link" title="Iron (Fe)">iron</a> in minerals such as  pentlandite and pyrrhotite with the formula Fe<sub>9−x</sub>Ni<sub>x</sub>S<sub>8</sub> and Fe<sub>7−x</sub>Ni<sub>x</sub>S<sub>6</sub>, respectively. Other common Ni-containing minerals are millerite and the arsenide  niccolite.
</p><p>Identified land-based resources throughout the world averaging 1% nickel or greater comprise at least 130&#160;million tons of nickel (about the double of known reserves). About 60% is in laterites and 40% in sulfide deposits.
</p><p>On geophysical evidence, most of the nickel on Earth is believed to be in Earth's outer and inner cores. Kamacite and taenite are naturally occurring alloys of <a href="/school1/ptable/element/Fe" class="wiki-link" title="Iron (Fe)">iron</a> and nickel. For kamacite, the alloy is usually in the proportion of 90:10 to 95:5, though impurities (such as <a href="/school1/ptable/element/Co" class="wiki-link" title="Cobalt (Co)">cobalt</a> or <a href="/school1/ptable/element/C" class="wiki-link" title="Carbon (C)">carbon</a>) may be present. Taenite is 20% to 65% nickel. Kamacite and taenite are also found in nickel iron meteorites.
</p><p>Nickel is commonly found in iron meteorites as the alloys kamacite and taenite. Nickel in meteorites was first detected in 1799 by Joseph-Louis Proust, a French chemist who then worked in Spain. Proust analyzed samples of the meteorite from Campo del Cielo (Argentina), which had been obtained in 1783 by Miguel Rubín de Celis, discovering the presence in them of nickel (about 10%) along with iron.
</p>
<div class="mw-heading mw-heading2">

=== History ===
</div>
<p>Unintentional use of <a href="/school1/ptable/element/Ni" class="wiki-link" title="Nickel (Ni)">nickel</a> can be traced back as far as 3500&#160;BCE. Bronzes from what is now Syria have been found to contain as much as 2% <a href="/school1/ptable/element/Ni" class="wiki-link" title="Nickel (Ni)">nickel</a>. Some ancient Chinese manuscripts suggest that "white <a href="/school1/ptable/element/Cu" class="wiki-link" title="Copper (Cu)">copper</a>" (cupronickel, known as <i>baitong</i>) was used there in 1700–1400&#160;BCE. This Paktong white <a href="/school1/ptable/element/Cu" class="wiki-link" title="Copper (Cu)">copper</a> was exported to Britain as early as the 17th century, but the <a href="/school1/ptable/element/Ni" class="wiki-link" title="Nickel (Ni)">nickel</a> content of this alloy was not discovered until 1822. Coins of nickel-<a href="/school1/ptable/element/Cu" class="wiki-link" title="Copper (Cu)">copper</a> alloy were minted by Bactrian kings Agathocles, Euthydemus II, and Pantaleon in the 2nd century BCE, possibly out of the Chinese cupronickel.
</p>
<figure class="mw-default-size" typeof="mw:File/Thumb"><img src="/school1/ptable/theme-assets/default/images/inline/04795ef517c561d3ef96e6c67b2046bc.jpg" decoding="async" width="250" height="182" class="mw-file-element"  data-file-width="2452" data-file-height="1789" /><figcaption>Nickeline/niccolite</figcaption></figure>
<p>In medieval Germany, a metallic yellow mineral was found in the Ore Mountains that resembled copper ore. But when miners were unable to get any copper from it, they blamed a mischievous sprite of German mythology, Nickel (similar to <i>Old Nick</i>), for besetting the copper. They called this ore <span title="German-language text"><i lang="de">Kupfernickel</i></span> from German <span title="German-language text"><i lang="de">Kupfer</i></span> 'copper'. This ore is now known as the mineral nickeline (formerly <i>niccolite</i>), a nickel arsenide. In 1751, Baron Axel Fredrik Cronstedt tried to extract copper from kupfernickel at a <a href="/school1/ptable/element/Co" class="wiki-link" title="Cobalt (Co)">cobalt</a> mine in the village of Los, Sweden, and instead produced a white metal that he named <i>nickel</i> after the spirit that had given its name to the mineral. In modern German, Kupfernickel or Kupfer-Nickel designates the alloy cupronickel.
</p><p>Originally, the only source for nickel was the rare Kupfernickel. Beginning in 1824, nickel was obtained as a byproduct of <a href="/school1/ptable/element/Co" class="wiki-link" title="Cobalt (Co)">cobalt</a> blue production. The first large-scale smelting of nickel began in Norway in 1848 from nickel-rich pyrrhotite. The introduction of nickel in steel production in 1889 increased the demand for nickel; the nickel deposits of New Caledonia, discovered in 1865, provided most of the world's supply between 1875 and 1915. The discovery of the large deposits in the Sudbury Basin in Canada in 1883, in Norilsk-Talnakh in Russia in 1920, and in the Merensky Reef in South Africa in 1924 made large-scale nickel production possible.
</p>
<div class="mw-heading mw-heading2">

=== Applications ===
</div>
<figure class="mw-default-size" typeof="mw:File/Thumb"><img src="/school1/ptable/theme-assets/default/images/inline/83fbf5ece5398c701713731eb4350bd2.jpg" decoding="async" width="250" height="453" class="mw-file-element"  data-file-width="587" data-file-height="1064" /><figcaption><a href="/school1/ptable/element/Ni" class="wiki-link" title="Nickel (Ni)">Nickel</a> foam (top) and its internal structure (bottom)</figcaption></figure>
<p>Global use of <a href="/school1/ptable/element/Ni" class="wiki-link" title="Nickel (Ni)">nickel</a> is currently 68% in stainless steel, 10% in nonferrous alloys, 9% electroplating, 7% alloy steel, 3% foundries, and 4% other (including batteries).
</p><p><a href="/school1/ptable/element/Ni" class="wiki-link" title="Nickel (Ni)">Nickel</a> is used in many recognizable industrial and consumer products, including stainless steel, alnico magnets, coinage, rechargeable batteries (e.g. nickel–<a href="/school1/ptable/element/Fe" class="wiki-link" title="Iron (Fe)">iron</a>), electric guitar strings, microphone capsules, plating on plumbing fixtures, and special alloys such as permalloy, elinvar, and invar. It is used for plating and as a green tint in glass. Nickel is preeminently an alloy metal, and its chief use is in nickel steels and nickel cast irons, in which it typically increases the tensile strength, toughness, and elastic limit. It is widely used in many other alloys, including nickel brasses and bronzes and alloys with <a href="/school1/ptable/element/Cu" class="wiki-link" title="Copper (Cu)">copper</a>, <a href="/school1/ptable/element/Cr" class="wiki-link" title="Chromium (Cr)">chromium</a>, aluminium, <a href="/school1/ptable/element/Pb" class="wiki-link" title="Lead (Pb)">lead</a>, <a href="/school1/ptable/element/Co" class="wiki-link" title="Cobalt (Co)">cobalt</a>, <a href="/school1/ptable/element/Ag" class="wiki-link" title="Silver (Ag)">silver</a>, and <a href="/school1/ptable/element/Au" class="wiki-link" title="Gold (Au)">gold</a> (Inconel, Incoloy, Monel, Nimonic).
</p><p>Nickel is traditionally used for Kris production in Southeast Asia.
</p>
<figure class="mw-default-size mw-halign-left" typeof="mw:File/Thumb"><img src="/school1/ptable/theme-assets/default/images/inline/d8a76ff81251c12a9a4a28cd3c63a5c9.jpg" decoding="async" width="250" height="255" class="mw-file-element"  data-file-width="470" data-file-height="480" /><figcaption>A "horseshoe magnet" made of alnico nickel alloy</figcaption></figure>
<p>Because nickel is resistant to corrosion, it was occasionally used as a substitute for decorative <a href="/school1/ptable/element/Ag" class="wiki-link" title="Silver (Ag)">silver</a>. Nickel was also occasionally used in some countries after 1859 as a cheap coinage metal (see above), but in the later years of the 20th century, it was replaced by cheaper stainless steel (i.e., <a href="/school1/ptable/element/Fe" class="wiki-link" title="Iron (Fe)">iron</a>) alloys, except in the United States and Canada.
</p><p>Nickel is an excellent alloying agent for certain precious metals and is used in the fire assay as a collector of <a href="/school1/ptable/element/Pt" class="wiki-link" title="Platinum (Pt)">platinum</a> group elements (PGE). As such, nickel can fully collect all six PGEs from ores, and can partially collect <a href="/school1/ptable/element/Au" class="wiki-link" title="Gold (Au)">gold</a>. High-throughput nickel mines may also do PGE recovery (mainly <a href="/school1/ptable/element/Pt" class="wiki-link" title="Platinum (Pt)">platinum</a> and <a href="/school1/ptable/element/Pd" class="wiki-link" title="Palladium (Pd)">palladium</a>); examples are Norilsk, Russia and the Sudbury Basin, Canada.
</p><p>Nickel foam or nickel mesh is used in gas diffusion electrodes for alkaline fuel cells.
</p><p>Nickel and its alloys are often used as catalysts for hydrogenation reactions. Raney nickel, a finely divided nickel-aluminium alloy, is one common form, though related catalysts are also used, including Raney-type catalysts.
</p><p>Nickel is naturally magnetostrictive: in the presence of a magnetic field, the material undergoes a small change in length. The magnetostriction of nickel is on the order of 50&#160;ppm and is negative, indicating that it contracts.
</p><p>Nickel is used as a binder in the cemented <a href="/school1/ptable/element/W" class="wiki-link" title="Tungsten (W)">tungsten</a> carbide or hardmetal industry and used in proportions of 6% to 12% by weight. Nickel makes the <a href="/school1/ptable/element/W" class="wiki-link" title="Tungsten (W)">tungsten</a> carbide magnetic and adds corrosion-resistance to the cemented parts, though the hardness is less than those with <a href="/school1/ptable/element/Co" class="wiki-link" title="Cobalt (Co)">cobalt</a> binder.
</p><p><span class="chemf nowrap"><span class="nowrap"><span style="display:inline-block;margin-bottom:-0.3em;vertical-align:0.5em;line-height:1em;font-size:80%;text-align:left"><sup style="font-size:inherit;line-height:inherit;vertical-align:baseline">63</sup><br /><sub style="font-size:inherit;line-height:inherit;vertical-align:baseline"></sub></span></span>Ni</span>, with a half-life of 100.1 years, is useful in krytron devices as a beta particle (high-speed electron) emitter to make ionization by the keep-alive electrode more reliable. It is being investigated as a power source for betavoltaic batteries.
</p><p>Around 27% of all nickel production is used for engineering, 10% for building and construction, 14% for tubular products, 20% for metal goods, 14% for transport, 11% for electronic goods, and 5% for other uses.
</p><p>In 2025, QuesTek Innovations and Stoke Space developed a nickel-based superalloy for additive manufacturing and extreme high-pressure, high-temperature <a href="/school1/ptable/element/O" class="wiki-link" title="Oxygen (O)">oxygen</a> environments. Its characteristics allow the material to be used for fully reusable spacecraft launch systems, it can withstand the full-flow staged combustion rocket engine Zenith.
</p><p>Raney nickel is widely used for hydrogenation of unsaturated oils to make margarine, and substandard margarine and leftover oil may contain nickel as a contaminant. Forte et al. found that type 2 diabetic patients have 0.89&#160;ng/mL of Ni in the blood relative to 0.77&#160;ng/mL in control subjects.
</p><p>Nickel <a href="/school1/ptable/element/Ti" class="wiki-link" title="Titanium (Ti)">titanium</a> is an alloy of roughly equal atomic percentages of its constituent metals which exhibits two closely related and unique properties: the shape memory effect and superelasticity.
</p>
<div class="mw-heading mw-heading2">

=== Biological role ===
</div>
<p>It was not recognized until the 1970s, but <a href="/school1/ptable/element/Ni" class="wiki-link" title="Nickel (Ni)">nickel</a> is known to play an important role in the biology of some plants, bacteria, archaea, and fungi. <a href="/school1/ptable/element/Ni" class="wiki-link" title="Nickel (Ni)">Nickel</a> enzymes such as urease are considered virulence factors in some organisms. Urease catalyzes hydrolysis of urea to form ammonia and carbamate. NiFe hydrogenases can catalyze oxidation of <link rel="mw-deduplicated-inline-style" href="mw-data:TemplateStyles:r1123817410" /><span class="chemf nowrap">H<sub class="template-chem2-sub">2</sub></span> to form protons and electrons; and also the reverse reaction, the reduction of protons to form <a href="/school1/ptable/element/H" class="wiki-link" title="Hydrogen (H)">hydrogen</a> gas. A <a href="/school1/ptable/element/Ni" class="wiki-link" title="Nickel (Ni)">nickel</a>-tetrapyrrole coenzyme, cofactor F430, is present in methyl coenzyme M reductase, which can catalyze the formation of methane, or the reverse reaction, in methanogenic archaea (in +1 oxidation state). One of the <a href="/school1/ptable/element/C" class="wiki-link" title="Carbon (C)">carbon</a> monoxide dehydrogenase enzymes consists of an Fe-Ni-S cluster. Other nickel-bearing enzymes include a rare bacterial class of superoxide dismutase and glyoxalase I enzymes in bacteria and several eukaryotic trypanosomal parasites (in other organisms, including yeast and mammals, this enzyme contains divalent <link rel="mw-deduplicated-inline-style" href="mw-data:TemplateStyles:r1123817410" /><span class="chemf nowrap">Zn<sup>2+</sup></span>).
</p><p>Dietary nickel may affect human health through infections by nickel-dependent bacteria, but nickel may also be an essential nutrient for bacteria living in the large intestine, in effect functioning as a prebiotic. The US Institute of Medicine has not confirmed that nickel is an essential nutrient for humans, so neither a Recommended Dietary Allowance (RDA) nor an Adequate Intake have been established. The tolerable upper intake level of dietary nickel is 1&#160;mg/day as soluble nickel salts. Estimated dietary intake is 70 to 100&#160;μg/day; less than 10% is absorbed. What is absorbed is excreted in urine. Relatively large amounts of nickel – comparable to the estimated average ingestion above – leach into food cooked in stainless steel. For example, the amount of nickel leached after 10 cooking cycles into one serving of tomato sauce averages 88&#160;μg.
</p><p>Nickel released from Siberian Traps volcanic eruptions is suspected of helping the growth of <i>Methanosarcina</i>, a genus of euryarchaeote archaea that produced methane in the Permian–Triassic extinction event, the biggest known mass extinction.
</p>
<div class="mw-heading mw-heading2">
