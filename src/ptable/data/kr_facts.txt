<p><b>Krypton</b> (from Ancient Greek: <span lang="grc">κρυπτός</span>, <small>romanized:&#160;</small><span title="Ancient Greek-language romanization"><i lang="grc-Latn">kryptos</i></span> <span class="gloss-quot">'</span><span class="gloss-text">the hidden one</span><span class="gloss-quot">'</span>) is a chemical element; it has symbol <b>Kr</b> and atomic number 36. It is a colorless, odorless noble gas that occurs in trace amounts in the atmosphere and is often used with other rare gases in fluorescent lamps. Krypton is chemically inert.
</p><p>Krypton, like the other noble gases, is used in lighting and photography. Krypton light has many spectral lines, and krypton plasma is useful in bright, high-powered gas lasers (krypton ion and excimer lasers), each of which resonates and amplifies a single spectral line. Krypton fluoride also makes a useful laser medium. From 1960 to 1983, the official definition of the metre was based on the wavelength of one spectral line of krypton-86, because of the high power and relative ease of operation of krypton discharge tubes.
</p>
<meta property="mw:PageProp/toc" />
<div class="mw-heading mw-heading2">

SECTIONS:
History:
</div>
<figure class="mw-default-size mw-halign-left" typeof="mw:File/Thumb"><img src="/school1/ptable/theme-assets/default/images/inline/c804572a9e0b3c0a5cb862b9e53b9076.jpg" decoding="async" width="190" height="291" style="--mw-file-upright: 0.75" class="mw-file-element mw-file-upright" srcset="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/d/d2/William_Ramsay_working.jpg/500px-William_Ramsay_working.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&amp;utm_campaign=parser&amp;utm_content=thumbnail 2x" data-file-width="800" data-file-height="1226" /><figcaption>Sir William Ramsay, the discoverer of <a href="/school1/ptable/element/Kr" class="wiki-link" title="Krypton (Kr)">krypton</a></figcaption></figure>
<p><a href="/school1/ptable/element/Kr" class="wiki-link" title="Krypton (Kr)">Krypton</a> was discovered in Britain in 1898 by William Ramsay, a Scottish chemist, and Morris Travers, an English chemist, in residue left from evaporating nearly all components of liquid air. <a href="/school1/ptable/element/Ne" class="wiki-link" title="Neon (Ne)">Neon</a> was discovered by a similar procedure by the same workers just a few weeks later. William Ramsay was awarded the 1904 Nobel Prize in Chemistry for discovery of a series of noble gases, including <a href="/school1/ptable/element/Kr" class="wiki-link" title="Krypton (Kr)">krypton</a>.
</p><p>In 1960, the International Bureau of Weights and Measures defined the meter as 1,650,763.73 wavelengths of light emitted in the vacuum corresponding to the transition between the 2p<sub>10</sub> and 5d<sub>5</sub> levels in the isotope krypton-86. This agreement replaced the 1889 international prototype meter, which was a metal bar located in Sèvres. This also made obsolete the 1927 definition of the ångström based on the red <a href="/school1/ptable/element/Cd" class="wiki-link" title="Cadmium (Cd)">cadmium</a> spectral line, replacing it with 1&#160;Å = 10<sup>−10</sup>&#160;m. The krypton-86 definition lasted until the October 1983 conference, which redefined the meter as the distance that light travels in vacuum during 1/299,792,458&#160;s.
</p>
<div class="mw-heading mw-heading2">

Characteristics:
</div>
<p><a href="/school1/ptable/element/Kr" class="wiki-link" title="Krypton (Kr)">Krypton</a> is characterized by several sharp emission lines (spectral signatures) the strongest being green and yellow. <a href="/school1/ptable/element/Kr" class="wiki-link" title="Krypton (Kr)">Krypton</a> is one of the products of <a href="/school1/ptable/element/U" class="wiki-link" title="Uranium (U)">uranium</a> fission. Solid <a href="/school1/ptable/element/Kr" class="wiki-link" title="Krypton (Kr)">krypton</a> is white and has a face-centered cubic crystal structure, which is a common property of all noble gases (except <a href="/school1/ptable/element/He" class="wiki-link" title="Helium (He)">helium</a>, which has a hexagonal close-packed crystal structure).
</p>
<div class="mw-heading mw-heading3">

Isotopes:
</div>
<link rel="mw-deduplicated-inline-style" href="mw-data:TemplateStyles:r1368532237" /><div role="note" class="hatnote navigation-not-searchable">Main article: Isotopes of <a href="/school1/ptable/element/Kr" class="wiki-link" title="Krypton (Kr)">krypton</a></div>
<p>Naturally occurring <a href="/school1/ptable/element/Kr" class="wiki-link" title="Krypton (Kr)">krypton</a> in Earth's atmosphere is composed of five stable isotopes, plus one isotope (<sup>78</sup>Kr) with such a long half-life (9.2×10<sup>21</sup>&#160;years) that it can be considered stable. (This isotope has the third-longest known half-life among all isotopes for which decay has been observed; it undergoes double electron capture to <sup>78</sup>Se). In addition, about thirty unstable isotopes and isomers are known. Traces of <sup>81</sup>Kr, a cosmogenic nuclide produced by the cosmic ray irradiation of <sup>80</sup>Kr, also occur in nature: this isotope is radioactive with a half-life of 230,000 years. <a href="/school1/ptable/element/Kr" class="wiki-link" title="Krypton (Kr)">Krypton</a> is highly volatile and does not stay in solution in near-surface water, but <sup>81</sup>Kr has been used for dating old (50,000–800,000 years) groundwater.
</p><p><sup>85</sup>Kr is an inert radioactive noble gas with a half-life of 10.76 years. It is produced by the fission of <a href="/school1/ptable/element/U" class="wiki-link" title="Uranium (U)">uranium</a> and <a href="/school1/ptable/element/Pu" class="wiki-link" title="Plutonium (Pu)">plutonium</a>, such as in nuclear bomb testing and nuclear reactors. <sup>85</sup>Kr is released during the reprocessing of fuel rods from nuclear reactors. Concentrations at the North Pole are 30% higher than at the South Pole due to convective mixing.
</p>
<div class="mw-heading mw-heading3">

Applications:
</div>
<figure class="mw-default-size mw-halign-left" typeof="mw:File/Thumb"><img src="/school1/ptable/theme-assets/default/images/inline/8aa6952edb0fb960f775d5d58d962c9e.jpg" decoding="async" width="250" height="250" class="mw-file-element" srcset="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/e/e7/KrTube.jpg/500px-KrTube.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&amp;utm_campaign=parser&amp;utm_content=thumbnail 2x" data-file-width="2000" data-file-height="2000" /><figcaption><a href="/school1/ptable/element/Kr" class="wiki-link" title="Krypton (Kr)">Krypton</a> gas discharge tube</figcaption></figure>
<p><a href="/school1/ptable/element/Kr" class="wiki-link" title="Krypton (Kr)">Krypton</a>'s multiple emission lines make ionized <a href="/school1/ptable/element/Kr" class="wiki-link" title="Krypton (Kr)">krypton</a> gas discharges appear whitish, which in turn makes krypton-based bulbs useful in photography as a white light source. Krypton is used in some photographic flashes for high speed photography. Krypton gas is also combined with <a href="/school1/ptable/element/Hg" class="wiki-link" title="Mercury (Hg)">mercury</a> to make luminous signs that glow with a bright greenish-blue light.
</p><p>Krypton is mixed with <a href="/school1/ptable/element/Ar" class="wiki-link" title="Argon (Ar)">argon</a> in energy efficient fluorescent lamps, reducing the power consumption, but also reducing the light output and raising the cost. Krypton costs about 100 times as much as <a href="/school1/ptable/element/Ar" class="wiki-link" title="Argon (Ar)">argon</a>. Krypton (along with <a href="/school1/ptable/element/Xe" class="wiki-link" title="Xenon (Xe)">xenon</a>) is also used to fill incandescent lamps to reduce filament evaporation and allow higher operating temperatures.
</p><p>Krypton's white discharge is sometimes used as an artistic effect in gas discharge "<a href="/school1/ptable/element/Ne" class="wiki-link" title="Neon (Ne)">neon</a>" tubes. Krypton produces much higher light power than <a href="/school1/ptable/element/Ne" class="wiki-link" title="Neon (Ne)">neon</a> in the red spectral line region, and for this reason, red lasers for high-power laser light-shows are often krypton lasers with mirrors that select the red spectral line for laser amplification and emission, rather than the more familiar <a href="/school1/ptable/element/He" class="wiki-link" title="Helium (He)">helium</a>-<a href="/school1/ptable/element/Ne" class="wiki-link" title="Neon (Ne)">neon</a> variety, which could not achieve the same multi-watt outputs.
</p><p>The krypton fluoride laser is important in nuclear fusion energy research in confinement experiments. The laser has high beam uniformity, short wavelength, and the spot size can be varied to track an imploding pellet.
</p><p>In experimental particle physics, liquid krypton is used to construct quasi-homogeneous electromagnetic calorimeters. A notable example is the calorimeter of the NA48 experiment at CERN containing about 27 tonnes of liquid krypton. This usage is rare, since liquid <a href="/school1/ptable/element/Ar" class="wiki-link" title="Argon (Ar)">argon</a> is less expensive. The advantage of krypton is a smaller Molière radius of 4.7&#160;cm, which provides excellent spatial resolution with little overlapping. The other parameters relevant for calorimetry are: radiation length of X<sub>0</sub>=4.7&#160;cm, and density of 2.4&#160;g/cm<sup>3</sup>.<sup class="noprint Inline-Template Template-Fact" style="white-space:nowrap;">&#91;<i><span title="This claim needs references to reliable sources. (March 2025)">citation needed</span></i>&#93;</sup>
</p><p>Krypton-83 has application in magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) for imaging airways. In particular, it enables the radiologist to distinguish between hydrophobic and hydrophilic surfaces containing an airway.
</p><p>Although <a href="/school1/ptable/element/Xe" class="wiki-link" title="Xenon (Xe)">xenon</a> has potential for use in computed tomography (CT) to assess regional ventilation, its anesthetic properties limit its fraction in the breathing gas to 35%. A breathing mixture of 30% <a href="/school1/ptable/element/Xe" class="wiki-link" title="Xenon (Xe)">xenon</a> and 30% krypton is comparable in effectiveness for CT to a 40% xenon fraction, while avoiding the unwanted effects of a high partial pressure of xenon gas. The metastable isotope krypton-81m is used in nuclear medicine for lung ventilation/perfusion scans, where it is inhaled and imaged with a gamma camera. Krypton-85 in the atmosphere has been used to detect clandestine nuclear fuel reprocessing facilities in North Korea and Pakistan. Those facilities were detected in the early 2000s and were believed to be producing weapons-grade <a href="/school1/ptable/element/Pu" class="wiki-link" title="Plutonium (Pu)">plutonium</a>. Krypton-85 is a medium lived fission product and thus escapes from spent fuel when the cladding is removed.
</p><p>Krypton is used occasionally as an insulating gas between window panes. SpaceX Starlink uses krypton as a propellant for their electric propulsion system.
