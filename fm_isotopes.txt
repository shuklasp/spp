<div style="clear:both;" class=""></div>
<p>There are 20 known isotopes of <a href="/school1/ptable/element/Fm" class="wiki-link" title="Fermium (Fm)">fermium</a>, with atomic weights of 241 to 260, of which <sup>257</sup>Fm is the longest-lived with a half-life of 100.5&#160;days. Other isotopes are considerably shorter: <sup>253</sup>Fm has a half-life of 3 days, <sup>252</sup>Fm of 25.4&#160;h, <sup>255</sup>Fm of 20.1&#160;h, while <sup>251</sup>Fm of 5.3&#160;h, <sup>254</sup>Fm of 3.2&#160;h, and <sup>256</sup>Fm of 2.67&#160;hours. All the remaining ones have half-lives ranging from 30 minutes to less than a millisecond.
</p><p>The neutron capture product of fermium-257, <sup>258</sup>Fm, undergoes spontaneous fission with a half-life of just 370(14)&#160;microseconds; <sup>259</sup>Fm and <sup>260</sup>Fm also undergo spontaneous fission (<i>t</i><sub>1/2</sub>&#160;= 1.5(3)&#160;s and 4&#160;ms respectively). This means that neutron capture does not create nuclides with a mass number greater than 257, unless carried out at extremely high flux, as in a nuclear explosion (or the astrophysical r-process), since no accessible fermium isotopes undergo beta minus decay to the next element, <a href="/school1/ptable/element/Md" class="wiki-link" title="Mendelevium (Md)">mendelevium</a>. Because of this impediment in forming heavier isotopes, these short-lived isotopes <sup>258–260</sup>Fm constitute the "fermium gap."
</p><p>Deliberate attempts at nuclear explosion synthesis, however, also failed to create heavier nuclei, and as a result were deemed not worth continuing.
</p>
