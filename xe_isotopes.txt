<link rel="mw-deduplicated-inline-style" href="mw-data:TemplateStyles:r1368532237" /><div role="note" class="hatnote navigation-not-searchable">Main article: Isotopes of <a href="/school1/ptable/element/Xe" class="wiki-link" title="Xenon (Xe)">xenon</a></div>
<p>Naturally occurring <a href="/school1/ptable/element/Xe" class="wiki-link" title="Xenon (Xe)">xenon</a> is composed of seven stable and two almost stable isotopes: <sup>126</sup>Xe, <sup>128–132</sup>Xe, and <sup>134</sup>Xe are stable, <sup>124</sup>Xe and <sup>136</sup>Xe have very long half-lives, trillions of times the age of the universe. The isotopes <sup>126</sup>Xe and <sup>134</sup>Xe are also predicted by theory to undergo double beta decay, but this has never been observed so they are considered stable.
</p><p>More than 40 unstable isotopes are known. The longest-lived of these isotopes are the primordial <sup>124</sup>Xe, which undergoes double electron capture with a half-life of <span class="nowrap"><span data-sort-value="7029347133600000000♠"></span>1.1<span style="margin-left:0.25em;margin-right:0.15em;">×</span>10<sup>22</sup>&#160;yr</span>, and <sup>136</sup>Xe, which undergoes double beta decay with a half-life of <span class="nowrap"><span data-sort-value="7028687955680000000♠"></span>2.18<span style="margin-left:0.25em;margin-right:0.15em;">×</span>10<sup>21</sup>&#160;yr</span>.
</p><p><sup>129</sup>Xe is produced by beta decay of <sup>129</sup>I, which has a half-life of 16.1&#160;million years. <sup>131m</sup>Xe, <sup>133</sup>Xe, <sup>133m</sup>Xe, and <sup>135</sup>Xe are some of the fission products of <sup>235</sup>U and <sup>239</sup>Pu, and are used to detect and monitor nuclear explosions.
</p>
