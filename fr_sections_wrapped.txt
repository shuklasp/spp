Characteristics:
<p><a href="/school1/ptable/element/Fr" class="wiki-link" title="Francium
(Fr)">Francium</a> is one of the most unstable of the naturally occurring
elements: its longest-lived isotope, <a href="/school1/ptable/element/Fr"
class="wiki-link" title="Francium (Fr)">francium</a>-223, has a half-life of
only 22&#160;minutes. The only comparable element is <a
href="/school1/ptable/element/At" class="wiki-link" title="Astatine
(At)">astatine</a>, whose most stable natural isotope, <a
href="/school1/ptable/element/At" class="wiki-link" title="Astatine
(At)">astatine</a>-219 (the alpha daughter of <a
href="/school1/ptable/element/Fr" class="wiki-link" title="Francium
(Fr)">francium</a>-223), has a half-life of 56&#160;seconds, although synthetic
<a href="/school1/ptable/element/At" class="wiki-link" title="Astatine
(At)">astatine</a>-210 is much longer-lived with a half-life of 8.1&#160;hours.
All isotopes of francium decay into astatine, <a
href="/school1/ptable/element/Ra" class="wiki-link" title="Radium
(Ra)">radium</a>, or <a href="/school1/ptable/element/Rn" class="wiki-link"
title="Radon (Rn)">radon</a>. Francium-223 also has a shorter half-life than the
longest-lived isotope known of each element up to and including element 105, <a
href="/school1/ptable/element/Db" class="wiki-link" title="Dubnium
(Db)">dubnium</a>. </p><p>Francium is an alkali metal whose chemical properties
mostly resemble those of caesium. A heavy element with a single valence
electron, it has the highest equivalent weight of any element. Liquid
francium—if created—should have a surface tension of 0.05092&#160;N/m at its
melting point. Francium's melting point was estimated to be around 8.0&#160;°C
(46.4&#160;°F); a value of 27&#160;°C (81&#160;°F) is also often encountered.
The melting point is uncertain because of the element's extreme rarity and
radioactivity; a different extrapolation based on Dmitri Mendeleev's method gave
20&#160;±&#160;1.5&#160;°C (68.0&#160;±&#160;2.7&#160;°F). A calculation based
on the melting temperatures of binary ionic crystals gives
24.861&#160;±&#160;0.517&#160;°C (76.750&#160;±&#160;0.931&#160;°F). The
estimated boiling point of 620&#160;°C (1,148&#160;°F) is also uncertain; the
estimates 598&#160;°C (1,108&#160;°F) and 677&#160;°C (1,251&#160;°F), as well
as the extrapolation from Mendeleev's method of 640&#160;°C (1,184&#160;°F),
have also been suggested. The density of francium is expected to be around
2.48&#160;g/cm<sup>3</sup> (Mendeleev's method extrapolates
2.4&#160;g/cm<sup>3</sup>). Another calculation gives a much higher value of
3.57&#160;g/cm<sup>3</sup>. Francium is predicted to have a bulk modulus of
2.1–2.6&#160;GPa. </p><p><span class="anchor"
id="electronegativity"></span>Linus Pauling estimated the electronegativity of
francium at 0.7 on the Pauling scale, the same as caesium; the value for caesium
has since been refined to 0.79, but there are no experimental data to allow a
refinement of the value for francium. Francium has a slightly higher ionization
energy than caesium, 392.811(4)&#160;kJ/mol as opposed to
375.7041(2)&#160;kJ/mol for caesium, as would be expected from relativistic
effects, and this would imply that caesium is the less electronegative of the
two. Francium should also have a higher electron affinity than caesium and the
Fr<sup>−</sup> ion should be more polarizable than the Cs<sup>−</sup> ion. </p>

Isotopes:

navigation-not-searchable">Main article: Isotopes of <a
href="/school1/ptable/element/Fr" class="wiki-link" title="Francium
(Fr)">francium</a></div> <p>There are 37 known isotopes of <a
href="/school1/ptable/element/Fr" class="wiki-link" title="Francium
(Fr)">francium</a> ranging in atomic mass from 197 to 233. <a
href="/school1/ptable/element/Fr" class="wiki-link" title="Francium
(Fr)">Francium</a> has seven metastable nuclear isomers. Francium-223 and
francium-221 are the only isotopes that occur in nature, with the former being
far more common. </p><p>Francium-223 is the most stable isotope, with a half-
life of 21.8&#160;minutes, and it is highly unlikely that an isotope of francium
with a longer half-life will ever be discovered or synthesized. Francium-223 is
a fifth product of the <a href="/school1/ptable/element/U" class="wiki-link"
title="Uranium (U)">uranium</a>-235 decay series as a daughter isotope of <a
href="/school1/ptable/element/Ac" class="wiki-link" title="Actinium
(Ac)">actinium</a>-227; <a href="/school1/ptable/element/Th" class="wiki-link"
title="Thorium (Th)">thorium</a>-227 is the more common daughter. Francium-223
then decays into <a href="/school1/ptable/element/Ra" class="wiki-link"
title="Radium (Ra)">radium</a>-223 by beta decay (1.149&#160;MeV decay energy),
with a minor (0.006%) alpha decay path to <a href="/school1/ptable/element/At"
class="wiki-link" title="Astatine (At)">astatine</a>-219 (5.4&#160;MeV decay
energy). </p><p>Francium-221 has a half-life of 4.8&#160;minutes. It is the
ninth product of the <a href="/school1/ptable/element/Np" class="wiki-link"
title="Neptunium (Np)">neptunium</a> decay series as a daughter isotope of <a
href="/school1/ptable/element/Ac" class="wiki-link" title="Actinium
(Ac)">actinium</a>-225. Francium-221 then decays into <a
href="/school1/ptable/element/At" class="wiki-link" title="Astatine
(At)">astatine</a>-217 by alpha decay (6.457&#160;MeV decay energy). Although
all primordial <sup>237</sup>Np is extinct, the <a
href="/school1/ptable/element/Np" class="wiki-link" title="Neptunium
(Np)">neptunium</a> decay series continues to exist naturally in tiny traces due
to (n,2n) knockout reactions in natural <sup>238</sup>U. Francium-222, with a
half-life of 14&#160;minutes, may be produced as a result of the beta decay of
natural <a href="/school1/ptable/element/Rn" class="wiki-link" title="Radon
(Rn)">radon</a>-222; this process has nonetheless not yet been observed, and it
is unknown whether this process is energetically possible. </p><p>The least
stable ground state isotope is francium-215, with a half-life of 90&#160;ns: it
undergoes a 9.54&#160;MeV alpha decay to <a href="/school1/ptable/element/At"
class="wiki-link" title="Astatine (At)">astatine</a>-211. </p> <div class="mw-
heading mw-heading2">

Applications:
<p>Due to its instability and rarity, there are no commercial applications for
<a href="/school1/ptable/element/Fr" class="wiki-link" title="Francium
(Fr)">francium</a>. It has been used for research purposes in the fields of
chemistry and of atomic structure. Its use as a potential diagnostic aid for
various cancers has also been explored, but this application has been deemed
impractical. </p><p><a href="/school1/ptable/element/Fr" class="wiki-link"
title="Francium (Fr)">Francium</a>'s ability to be synthesized, trapped, and
cooled, along with its relatively simple atomic structure, has made it the
subject of specialized spectroscopy experiments. These experiments have led to
more specific information regarding energy levels and the coupling constants
between subatomic particles. Studies on the light emitted by laser-trapped <a
href="/school1/ptable/element/Fr" class="wiki-link" title="Francium
(Fr)">francium</a>-210 ions have provided accurate data on transitions between
atomic energy levels which are fairly similar to those predicted by quantum
theory. Francium is a prospective candidate for searching for CP violation. </p>

History:
<p>As early as 1870, chemists thought that there should be an alkali metal
beyond caesium, with an atomic number of 87. It was then referred to by the
provisional name <i>eka-caesium</i>. </p> <div class="mw-heading mw-heading3">
