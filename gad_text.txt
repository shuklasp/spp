<p><b>Gadolinium</b> is a chemical element; it has symbol <b>Gd</b> and atomic number 64. It is a silvery-white metal when oxidation is removed. Gadolinium is a ductile and malleable rare-earth element. It reacts with atmospheric <a href="/school1/ptable/element/O" class="wiki-link" title="Oxygen (O)">oxygen</a> or moisture slowly to form a black oxide coating. Gadolinium below its Curie point of 20&#160;°C (68&#160;°F) is ferromagnetic, with an attraction to a magnetic field higher than that of <a href="/school1/ptable/element/Ni" class="wiki-link" title="Nickel (Ni)">nickel</a>. Above this temperature it is the most paramagnetic element. It is found in nature only in an oxidized form. When separated, it usually has impurities of the other rare earths because of their similar chemical properties.
</p><p>Gadolinium was discovered in 1880 by Jean Charles de Marignac, who detected its oxide by using spectroscopy. It is named after the mineral gadolinite, one of the minerals in which gadolinium is found, itself named for the Finnish chemist Johan Gadolin. Pure gadolinium was first isolated by the chemist Félix Trombe in 1935.
</p><p>Gadolinium possesses unusual metallurgical properties, to the extent that as little as 1% of gadolinium can significantly improve the workability and resistance to oxidation at high temperatures of <a href="/school1/ptable/element/Fe" class="wiki-link" title="Iron (Fe)">iron</a>, <a href="/school1/ptable/element/Cr" class="wiki-link" title="Chromium (Cr)">chromium</a>, and related metals. Gadolinium as a metal or a salt absorbs neutrons and is, therefore, used sometimes for shielding in neutron radiography and in nuclear reactors.
</p><p>Like most of the rare earths, gadolinium forms trivalent ions with fluorescent properties, and salts of gadolinium(III) are used as phosphors in various applications.
</p><p>Gadolinium(III) ions in water-soluble salts are highly toxic to mammals. However, chelated gadolinium(III) compounds prevent the gadolinium(III) from being exposed to the organism, and the majority is excreted by healthy kidneys before it can deposit in tissues. Because of its paramagnetic properties, solutions of chelated organic gadolinium complexes are used as intravenously administered gadolinium-based MRI contrast agents in medical magnetic resonance imaging.
</p><p>The main uses of gadolinium, in addition to use as a contrast agent for MRI scans, are in nuclear reactors, in alloys, as a phosphor in medical imaging, as a gamma ray emitter, in electronic devices, in optical devices, and in superconductors.
</p>

--- Physical properties ---
<p><a href="/school1/ptable/element/Gd" class="wiki-link" title="Gadolinium (Gd)">Gadolinium</a> is the eighth member of the lanthanide series. In the periodic table, it appears between the elements <a href="/school1/ptable/element/Eu" class="wiki-link" title="Europium (Eu)">europium</a> to its left and <a href="/school1/ptable/element/Tb" class="wiki-link" title="Terbium (Tb)">terbium</a> to its right, and above the actinide <a href="/school1/ptable/element/Cm" class="wiki-link" title="Curium (Cm)">curium</a>. It is a silvery-white, malleable, ductile rare-earth element. Its 64 electrons are arranged in the configuration of [Xe]4f<sup>7</sup>5d<sup>1</sup>6s<sup>2</sup>, of which the ten 4f, 5d, and 6s electrons are valence.
</p><p>Like most other metals in the lanthanide series, three electrons are usually available as valence electrons. The remaining 4f electrons are too strongly bound: this is because the 4f orbitals penetrate the most through the inert <a href="/school1/ptable/element/Xe" class="wiki-link" title="Xenon (Xe)">xenon</a> core of electrons to the nucleus, followed by 5d and 6s, and this increases with higher ionic charge. <a href="/school1/ptable/element/Gd" class="wiki-link" title="Gadolinium (Gd)">Gadolinium</a> crystallizes in the hexagonal close-packed α-form at room temperature.   At temperatures above 1,235&#160;°C (2,255&#160;°F), it forms or transforms into its β-form, which has a body-centered cubic structure.
</p><p>The isotope <a href="/school1/ptable/element/Gd" class="wiki-link" title="Gadolinium (Gd)">gadolinium</a>-157 has the highest thermal-neutron capture cross-section among any stable nuclide: about 259,000 barns. Only <a href="/school1/ptable/element/Xe" class="wiki-link" title="Xenon (Xe)">xenon</a>-135 has a higher capture cross-section, about 2.0&#160;million barns, but is radioactive.
</p><p>Gadolinium is believed to be ferromagnetic at temperatures below 20&#160;°C (68&#160;°F) and is strongly paramagnetic above this temperature. In fact, at body temperature, gadolinium exhibits the greatest paramagnetic effect of any element. There is evidence that gadolinium is a helical antiferromagnetic, rather than a ferromagnetic, below 20&#160;°C (68&#160;°F). Gadolinium demonstrates a magnetocaloric effect whereby its temperature increases when it enters a magnetic field and decreases when it leaves the magnetic field. A significant magnetocaloric effect is observed at higher temperatures, up to about 300&#160;kelvins, in the compounds Gd<sub>5</sub>(Si<sub>1−<i>x</i></sub>Ge<sub><i>x</i></sub>)<sub>4</sub>.
</p><p>Individual gadolinium atoms can be isolated by encapsulating them into fullerene molecules, where they can be visualized with a transmission electron microscope. Individual Gd atoms and small Gd clusters can be incorporated into <a href="/school1/ptable/element/C" class="wiki-link" title="Carbon (C)">carbon</a> nanotubes.
</p>
<div class="mw-heading mw-heading3">

--- Chemical properties ---
<link rel="mw-deduplicated-inline-style" href="mw-data:TemplateStyles:r1368532237" /><div role="note" class="hatnote navigation-not-searchable selfref">See also: Category:<a href="/school1/ptable/element/Gd" class="wiki-link" title="Gadolinium (Gd)">Gadolinium</a> compounds</div>
<p><a href="/school1/ptable/element/Gd" class="wiki-link" title="Gadolinium (Gd)">Gadolinium</a> combines with most elements to form Gd(III) derivatives. It also combines with <a href="/school1/ptable/element/N" class="wiki-link" title="Nitrogen (N)">nitrogen</a>, <a href="/school1/ptable/element/C" class="wiki-link" title="Carbon (C)">carbon</a>, <a href="/school1/ptable/element/S" class="wiki-link" title="Sulfur (S)">sulfur</a>, <a href="/school1/ptable/element/P" class="wiki-link" title="Phosphorus (P)">phosphorus</a>, <a href="/school1/ptable/element/B" class="wiki-link" title="Boron (B)">boron</a>, <a href="/school1/ptable/element/Se" class="wiki-link" title="Selenium (Se)">selenium</a>, <a href="/school1/ptable/element/Si" class="wiki-link" title="Silicon (Si)">silicon</a>, and <a href="/school1/ptable/element/As" class="wiki-link" title="Arsenic (As)">arsenic</a> at elevated temperatures, forming binary compounds.
</p><p>Unlike the other rare-earth elements, metallic <a href="/school1/ptable/element/Gd" class="wiki-link" title="Gadolinium (Gd)">gadolinium</a> is relatively stable in dry air. However, it tarnishes quickly in moist air, forming a loosely-adhering gadolinium(III) oxide (<style data-mw-deduplicate="TemplateStyles:r1123817410">.mw-parser-output .template-chem2-su{display:inline-block;font-size:80%;line-height:1;vertical-align:-0.35em}.mw-parser-output .template-chem2-su>span{display:block;text-align:left}.mw-parser-output sub.template-chem2-sub{font-size:80%;vertical-align:-0.35em}.mw-parser-output sup.template-chem2-sup{font-size:80%;vertical-align:0.65em}</style><span class="chemf nowrap">Gd<sub class="template-chem2-sub">2</sub>O<sub class="template-chem2-sub">3</sub></span>):
</p>
<dl><dd><link rel="mw-deduplicated-inline-style" href="mw-data:TemplateStyles:r1123817410" /><span class="chemf nowrap">4 Gd + 3 O<sub class="template-chem2-sub">2</sub> → 2 Gd<sub class="template-chem2-sub">2</sub>O<sub class="template-chem2-sub">3</sub></span>,</dd></dl>
<p>which spalls off, exposing more surface to oxidation.
</p><p>Gadolinium is a strong reducing agent, which reduces oxides of several metals into their elements. Gadolinium is quite electropositive and reacts slowly with cold water and quite quickly with hot water to form gadolinium(III) hydroxide (<link rel="mw-deduplicated-inline-style" href="mw-data:TemplateStyles:r1123817410" /><span class="chemf nowrap">Gd(OH)<sub class="template-chem2-sub">3</sub></span>):
</p>
<dl><dd><link rel="mw-deduplicated-inline-style" href="mw-data:TemplateStyles:r1123817410" /><span class="chemf nowrap">2 Gd + 6 H<sub class="template-chem2-sub">2</sub>O → 2 Gd(OH)<sub class="template-chem2-sub">3</sub> + 3 H<sub class="template-chem2-sub">2</sub></span>.</dd></dl>
<p>Gadolinium metal is attacked readily by dilute sulfuric acid to form solutions containing the colorless Gd(III) ions, which exist as <link rel="mw-deduplicated-inline-style" href="mw-data:TemplateStyles:r1123817410" /><span class="chemf nowrap">&#91;Gd(H<sub class="template-chem2-sub">2</sub>O)<sub class="template-chem2-sub">9</sub>]<sup>3+</sup></span> complexes:
</p>
<dl><dd><link rel="mw-deduplicated-inline-style" href="mw-data:TemplateStyles:r1123817410" /><span class="chemf nowrap">2 Gd + 3 H<sub class="template-chem2-sub">2</sub>SO<sub class="template-chem2-sub">4</sub> + 18 H<sub class="template-chem2-sub">2</sub>O → 2 &#91;Gd(H<sub class="template-chem2-sub">2</sub>O)<sub class="template-chem2-sub">9</sub>]<sup>3+</sup> + 3 SO<span class="template-chem2-su"><span>2−</span><span>4</span></span> + 3 H<sub class="template-chem2-sub">2</sub></span>.</dd></dl>

--- Isotopes ---
<link rel="mw-deduplicated-inline-style" href="mw-data:TemplateStyles:r1368532237" /><div role="note" class="hatnote navigation-not-searchable">Main article: Isotopes of <a href="/school1/ptable/element/Gd" class="wiki-link" title="Gadolinium (Gd)">gadolinium</a></div>
<p>Naturally occurring <a href="/school1/ptable/element/Gd" class="wiki-link" title="Gadolinium (Gd)">gadolinium</a> is composed of the element's six stable isotopes—<sup>154</sup>Gd, <sup>155</sup>Gd, <sup>156</sup>Gd, <sup>157</sup>Gd, <sup>158</sup>Gd, and <sup>160</sup>Gd—as well as the primordial radionuclide <sup>152</sup>Gd, which has an extremely long half-life of 1.08×10<sup>14</sup> years. Out of these isotopes, <sup>158</sup>Gd has the highest natural abundance at 24.8%.
</p><p><a href="/school1/ptable/element/Gd" class="wiki-link" title="Gadolinium (Gd)">Gadolinium</a> has 35 known radioisotopes, ranging in mass number from 133 to 173. The three most stable of these all undergo alpha decay into isotopes of <a href="/school1/ptable/element/Sm" class="wiki-link" title="Samarium (Sm)">samarium</a>: <sup>152</sup>Gd (t<sub>½</sub> = 1.08×10<sup>14</sup> years), <sup>150</sup>Gd (t<sub>½</sub> = 1.79×10<sup>6</sup> years), and <sup>148</sup>Gd (t<sub>½</sub> = 86.9 years). All remaining radioisotopes have half-lives less than a year, and the majority have half-lives less than two minutes. Radioisotopes of gadolinium with mass numbers at or below 147 primarily decay via electron capture into isotopes of <a href="/school1/ptable/element/Eu" class="wiki-link" title="Europium (Eu)">europium</a>, while those with mass numbers at or above 161 primarily decay via beta minus decay into isotopes of <a href="/school1/ptable/element/Tb" class="wiki-link" title="Terbium (Tb)">terbium</a>. Gadolinium also has fourteen known metastable isomers, with the three most stable being <sup>143m</sup>Gd (t<sub>½</sub> = 110 seconds), <sup>145m</sup>Gd (t<sub>½</sub> = 85 seconds), and <sup>141m</sup>Gd (t<sub>½</sub> = 24.5 seconds).
</p><p><sup>154</sup>Gd, <sup>155</sup>Gd, and <sup>160</sup>Gd are observationally stable, meaning that they are predicted to decay, but their decay has never been observed. <sup>154</sup>Gd and <sup>155</sup>Gd are expected to alpha decay into <sup>150</sup>Sm and <sup>151</sup>Sm respectively, while <sup>160</sup>Gd is expected to double beta decay into <sup>160</sup>Dy. An experimental lower bound for the half-life of <sup>160</sup>Gd has been measured to be 1.3×10<sup>21</sup> years.
</p>

--- History ---
<figure class="mw-default-size" typeof="mw:File/Thumb"><img alt="Black and white bust painting of a young man with neckerchief in a coat. The hair is only faintly painted and looks grey." src="/school1/ptable/theme-assets/default/images/inline/55c9e4529a516841a3ee10c4bd311c07.jpg" decoding="async" width="180" height="218" style="--mw-file-upright: 0.7" class="mw-file-element mw-file-upright" srcset="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/f/fb/Johan_Gadolin.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&amp;utm_campaign=parser&amp;utm_content=thumbnail_unscaled 2x" data-file-width="422" data-file-height="512" /><figcaption>Johan Gadolin is the namesake of gadolinite, the mineral that <a href="/school1/ptable/element/Gd" class="wiki-link" title="Gadolinium (Gd)">gadolinium</a> is named after</figcaption></figure><p> In 1794, the Finnish chemist and mineralogist Johan Gadolin became the first to chemically analyze the mineral now known as gadolinite, which was named after him by the German chemist Martin Klaproth in 1802. In 1880, the Swiss chemist Jean Charles Galissard de Marignac observed the spectroscopic lines of a then-unknown element, <a href="/school1/ptable/element/Gd" class="wiki-link" title="Gadolinium (Gd)">gadolinium</a>, in samples of gadolinite (which actually contains relatively little <a href="/school1/ptable/element/Gd" class="wiki-link" title="Gadolinium (Gd)">gadolinium</a>, but enough to show a spectrum) and the separate mineral cerite, which proved to contain far more of the new element. De Marignac eventually separated a mineral oxide from cerite, which he realized was the oxide of the element. He designated the element with the provisional symbol Yα. The French chemist Paul-Émile Lecoq de Boisbaudran named the element "gadolinium" after gadolinite in 1886. Pure gadolinium metal was isolated for the first time in 1935 by Félix Trombe.
</p><div class="mw-heading mw-heading2">

--- Occurrence ---
<figure class="mw-default-size" typeof="mw:File/Thumb"><img src="/school1/ptable/theme-assets/default/images/inline/05977aef838e413c0fdb741634c7e3bf.jpg" decoding="async" width="250" height="188" class="mw-file-element" srcset="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/1/1b/Gadolinitas.jpg/500px-Gadolinitas.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&amp;utm_campaign=parser&amp;utm_content=thumbnail 2x" data-file-width="800" data-file-height="600" /><figcaption>Gadolinite</figcaption></figure>
<p><a href="/school1/ptable/element/Gd" class="wiki-link" title="Gadolinium (Gd)">Gadolinium</a> is a constituent in many minerals, such as monazite and bastnäsite. The metal is too reactive to exist naturally. Paradoxically, as noted above, the mineral gadolinite actually contains only traces of this element. The abundance in the Earth's crust is about 6.2&#160;mg/kg. The main mining areas are in China, the US, Brazil, Sri Lanka, India, and Australia with reserves expected to exceed one million tonnes. World production of pure <a href="/school1/ptable/element/Gd" class="wiki-link" title="Gadolinium (Gd)">gadolinium</a> is about 400 tonnes per year. The only known mineral with essential <a href="/school1/ptable/element/Gd" class="wiki-link" title="Gadolinium (Gd)">gadolinium</a>, lepersonnite-(Gd), is very rare.
</p>

<p><a href="/school1/ptable/element/Gd" class="wiki-link" title="Gadolinium (Gd)">Gadolinium</a> has no large-scale applications, but it has a variety of specialized uses.
