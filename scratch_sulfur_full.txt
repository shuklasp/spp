=== SECTION ===
Physical properties:
</div>
<figure class="mw-default-size mw-halign-left" typeof="mw:File/Thumb"><img src="/school1/ptable/theme-assets/default/images/inline/fbeef40435b8c6e1d603791874012321.png" decoding="async" width="190" height="228" style="--mw-file-upright: 0.75" class="mw-file-element mw-file-upright"  data-file-width="440" data-file-height="528" /><figcaption>As a solid, <a href="/school1/ptable/element/S" class="wiki-link" title="Sulfur (S)">sulfur</a> is a characteristic lemon yellow; when burned, <a href="/school1/ptable/element/S" class="wiki-link" title="Sulfur (S)">sulfur</a> melts into a blood-red liquid and emits a blue flame.</figcaption></figure>
<p><a href="/school1/ptable/element/S" class="wiki-link" title="Sulfur (S)">Sulfur</a> forms several polyatomic molecules. The best-known allotrope is octasulfur, cyclo-S<sub>8</sub>. The point group of cyclo-S<sub>8</sub> is D<sub>4d</sub> and its dipole moment is 0 D. Octasulfur is a soft, bright-yellow solid that is odorless. It melts at 115.21&#160;°C (239.38&#160;°F), and boils at 444.6&#160;°C (832.3&#160;°F). At 95.2&#160;°C (203.4&#160;°F), below its melting temperature, cyclo-octasulfur begins slowly changing from α-octasulfur to the β-polymorph. The structure of the S<sub>8</sub> ring is virtually unchanged by this phase transition, which affects the intermolecular interactions. Cooling molten sulfur freezes at 119.6&#160;°C (247.3&#160;°F), as it predominantly consists of the β-S<sub>8</sub> molecules. Between its melting and boiling temperatures, octasulfur changes its allotrope again, turning from β-octasulfur to γ-sulfur, again accompanied by a lower density but increased viscosity due to the formation of polymers. At higher temperatures, the viscosity decreases as depolymerization occurs. Molten sulfur assumes a dark red color above 200&#160;°C (392&#160;°F). The density of sulfur is about 2&#160;g/cm<sup>3</sup>, depending on the allotrope; all of the stable allotropes are excellent electrical insulators.
</p><p>The sublimation of sulfur becomes noticeable more or less between 20&#160;°C (68&#160;°F) and 50&#160;°C (122&#160;°F), and occurs readily in boiling water at 100&#160;°C (212&#160;°F).
</p><p>Sulfur is insoluble in water but soluble in <a href="/school1/ptable/element/C" class="wiki-link" title="Carbon (C)">carbon</a> disulfide and, to a lesser extent, in other nonpolar organic solvents, such as benzene and toluene. Sulfur is also soluble in supercritical <a href="/school1/ptable/element/C" class="wiki-link" title="Carbon (C)">carbon</a> dioxide.
</p>

<p>The reaction involves adsorption of protons onto <span class="chemf nowrap">S<span class="nowrap"><span style="display:inline-block;margin-bottom:-0.3em;vertical-align:-0.4em;line-height:1em;font-size:80%;text-align:left"><sup style="font-size:inherit;line-height:inherit;vertical-align:baseline"></sup><br /><sub style="font-size:inherit;line-height:inherit;vertical-align:baseline">8</sub></span></span></span> clusters, followed by disproportionation into the reaction products.
</p><p>The second, fourth and sixth ionization energies of <a href="/school1/ptable/element/S" class="wiki-link" title="Sulfur (S)">sulfur</a> are 2252&#160;kJ/mol, 4556&#160;kJ/mol and 8495.8&#160;kJ/mol, respectively. The composition of reaction products of <a href="/school1/ptable/element/S" class="wiki-link" title="Sulfur (S)">sulfur</a> with oxidants (and its oxidation state) depends on whether releasing of reaction energy overcomes these thresholds. Applying catalysts and/or supply of external energy may vary sulfur's oxidation state and the composition of reaction products. While reaction between sulfur and <a href="/school1/ptable/element/O" class="wiki-link" title="Oxygen (O)">oxygen</a> under normal conditions gives sulfur dioxide (oxidation state +4), formation of sulfur trioxide (oxidation state +6) requires a temperature of 400–600&#160;°C (750–1,100&#160;°F) and presence of a catalyst.
</p><p>In reactions with elements of lesser electronegativity, it reacts as an oxidant and forms sulfides, where it has oxidation state −2.
</p><p>Sulfur reacts with nearly all other elements except noble gases, even with the notoriously unreactive metal <a href="/school1/ptable/element/Ir" class="wiki-link" title="Iridium (Ir)">iridium</a> (yielding <a href="/school1/ptable/element/Ir" class="wiki-link" title="Iridium (Ir)">iridium</a> disulfide). Some of those reactions require elevated temperatures.
</p>

=== SECTION ===
Isotopes:
</div>
<link rel="mw-deduplicated-inline-style" href="mw-data:TemplateStyles:r1368532237" /><div role="note" class="hatnote navigation-not-searchable">Main article: Isotopes of <a href="/school1/ptable/element/S" class="wiki-link" title="Sulfur (S)">sulfur</a></div>
<p><a href="/school1/ptable/element/S" class="wiki-link" title="Sulfur (S)">Sulfur</a> has 23 known isotopes, four of which are stable: <sup>32</sup>S (<span class="nowrap"><span data-sort-value="6999949900000000000♠"></span>94.99%<span style="margin-left:0.225em;margin-right:0.225em;">±</span>0.26%</span>), <sup>33</sup>S (<span class="nowrap"><span data-sort-value="6997750000000000000♠"></span>0.75%<span style="margin-left:0.225em;margin-right:0.225em;">±</span>0.02%</span>), <sup>34</sup>S (<span class="nowrap"><span data-sort-value="6998425000000000000♠"></span>4.25%<span style="margin-left:0.225em;margin-right:0.225em;">±</span>0.24%</span>), and <sup>36</sup>S (<span class="nowrap"><span data-sort-value="6996100000000000000♠"></span>0.01%<span style="margin-left:0.225em;margin-right:0.225em;">±</span>0.01%</span>). Other than <sup>35</sup>S, with a half-life of 87.37 days, the radioactive isotopes of <a href="/school1/ptable/element/S" class="wiki-link" title="Sulfur (S)">sulfur</a> have half-lives less than 3&#160;hours.
</p><p>The preponderance of <sup>32</sup>S is explained by its production in the alpha process (one of the main classes of nuclear fusion reactions) in exploding stars. Other stable sulfur isotopes are produced in the bypass processes related with <sup>34</sup>Ar <sup class="noprint Inline-Template" style="margin-left:0.1em; white-space:nowrap;">&#91;<i><span title="The text near this tag may need clarification or removal of jargon. (July 2025)">clarification needed</span></i>&#93;</sup>, and their composition depends on the type of a stellar explosion. For example, proportionally more <sup>33</sup>S comes from novae than from supernovae.
</p><p>It has been found that the proportion of the two most abundant sulfur isotopes <sup>32</sup>S and <sup>34</sup>S varies in different samples by a surprisingly large amount. Determination of the isotope ratio (δ<sup>34</sup>S) in the samples indicates their chemical history, and with support of other methods, it allows to age-date the samples, estimate temperature of equilibrium between ore and water, determine pH and <a href="/school1/ptable/element/O" class="wiki-link" title="Oxygen (O)">oxygen</a> fugacity, identify the activity of sulfate-reducing bacteria in the time of formation of the sample, or suggest the main sources of sulfur in ecosystems. However, there are ongoing discussions over the real reason for the δ<sup>34</sup>S shifts, biological activity or postdeposit alteration.
</p><p>For example, when sulfide minerals are precipitated, isotopic equilibration between solid and liquid may cause small differences in the δ<sup>34</sup>S values of co-genetic minerals. The differences between minerals can be used to estimate the temperature of equilibration. The δ<sup>13</sup>C and δ<sup>34</sup>S of coexisting carbonate minerals and sulfides can be used to determine the pH and <a href="/school1/ptable/element/O" class="wiki-link" title="Oxygen (O)">oxygen</a> fugacity of the ore-bearing fluid during ore formation.
</p><p>Scientists measure the sulfur isotopes of minerals in rocks and sediments to study the redox conditions in past oceans. Sulfate-reducing bacteria in marine sediment fractionate sulfur isotopes as they take in sulfate and produce sulfide. Prior to the 2010s, it was thought that sulfate reduction could fractionate sulfur isotopes up to 46 permil and fractionation larger than 46 permil recorded in sediments must be due to disproportionation of sulfur compounds in the sediment. This view has changed since the 2010s as experiments showed that sulfate-reducing bacteria can fractionate to 66 permil. As substrates for disproportionation are limited by the product of sulfate reduction, the isotopic effect of disproportionation should be less than 16 permil in most sedimentary settings.
</p><p>In forest ecosystems, sulfate is derived mostly from the atmosphere; weathering of ore minerals and evaporites contribute some sulfur. Sulfur with a distinctive isotopic composition has been used to identify pollution sources, and enriched sulfur has been added as a tracer in hydrologic studies. Differences in the natural abundances can be used in systems where there is sufficient variation in the <sup>34</sup>S of ecosystem components. Rocky Mountain lakes thought to be dominated by atmospheric sources of sulfate have been found to have measurably different <sup>34</sup>S values than lakes believed to be dominated by watershed sources of sulfate.
</p><p>The radioactive <sup>35</sup>S is formed in cosmic ray spallation of the atmospheric <sup>40</sup>Ar. This fact may be used to verify the presence of recent (less than a year old) atmospheric sediments in various materials. This isotope may be obtained artificially in different ways. In practice, the reaction <sup>35</sup>Cl + n → <sup>35</sup>S + p is used, irradiating <a href="/school1/ptable/element/K" class="wiki-link" title="Potassium (K)">potassium</a> chloride with neutrons. The isotope <sup>35</sup>S is used in various sulfur-containing compounds as a radioactive tracer for many biological studies, for example, the Hershey-Chase experiment.
</p><p>Because of the weak beta activity of <sup>35</sup>S, its compounds are relatively safe as long as they are not ingested or absorbed by the body.
</p>

=== SECTION ===
Biological role:
</div>
<p><a href="/school1/ptable/element/S" class="wiki-link" title="Sulfur (S)">Sulfur</a> is an essential component of all living cells. It is the eighth most abundant element in the human body by weight, about equal in abundance to <a href="/school1/ptable/element/K" class="wiki-link" title="Potassium (K)">potassium</a>, and slightly greater than <a href="/school1/ptable/element/Na" class="wiki-link" title="Sodium (Na)">sodium</a> and <a href="/school1/ptable/element/Cl" class="wiki-link" title="Chlorine (Cl)">chlorine</a>. A 70&#160;kg (150&#160;lb) human body contains about 140 grams (4.9&#160;oz) of <a href="/school1/ptable/element/S" class="wiki-link" title="Sulfur (S)">sulfur</a>. The main dietary source of <a href="/school1/ptable/element/S" class="wiki-link" title="Sulfur (S)">sulfur</a> for humans is sulfur-containing amino acids, which can be found in plant and animal proteins.
</p>
