--- Physical properties ---
<p>Pure <a href="/school1/ptable/element/Tm" class="wiki-link" title="Thulium (Tm)">thulium</a> metal has a bright, silvery luster, which tarnishes on exposure to air. The metal can be cut with a knife, as it has a Mohs hardness of 2 to 3; it is malleable and ductile. <a href="/school1/ptable/element/Tm" class="wiki-link" title="Thulium (Tm)">Thulium</a> is ferromagnetic below 32<span class="nowrap">&#160;</span>K, antiferromagnetic between 32 and 56<span class="nowrap">&#160;</span>K, and paramagnetic above 56<span class="nowrap">&#160;</span>K.
</p><p><a href="/school1/ptable/element/Tm" class="wiki-link" title="Thulium (Tm)">Thulium</a> has two major allotropes: the tetragonal α-Tm and the more stable hexagonal β-Tm.
</p>
<div class="mw-heading mw-heading3">

--- Chemical properties ---
<p><a href="/school1/ptable/element/Tm" class="wiki-link" title="Thulium (Tm)">Thulium</a> tarnishes slowly in air and burns readily at 150<span class="nowrap">&#160;</span>°C to form <a href="/school1/ptable/element/Tm" class="wiki-link" title="Thulium (Tm)">thulium</a>(III) oxide:
</p>
<dl><dd><style data-mw-deduplicate="TemplateStyles:r1123817410">.mw-parser-output .template-chem2-su{display:inline-block;font-size:80%;line-height:1;vertical-align:-0.35em}.mw-parser-output .template-chem2-su>span{display:block;text-align:left}.mw-parser-output sub.template-chem2-sub{font-size:80%;vertical-align:-0.35em}.mw-parser-output sup.template-chem2-sup{font-size:80%;vertical-align:0.65em}</style><span class="chemf nowrap">4 Tm + 3 O<sub class="template-chem2-sub">2</sub> → 2 Tm<sub class="template-chem2-sub">2</sub>O<sub class="template-chem2-sub">3</sub></span></dd></dl>

--- Isotopes ---
<link rel="mw-deduplicated-inline-style" href="mw-data:TemplateStyles:r1368532237" /><div role="note" class="hatnote navigation-not-searchable">Main article: Isotopes of <a href="/school1/ptable/element/Tm" class="wiki-link" title="Thulium (Tm)">thulium</a></div>
<p>Natural <a href="/school1/ptable/element/Tm" class="wiki-link" title="Thulium (Tm)">thulium</a> consists of the single observationally stable isotope <a href="/school1/ptable/element/Tm" class="wiki-link" title="Thulium (Tm)">thulium</a>-169, which is predicted to undergo alpha decay to <a href="/school1/ptable/element/Ho" class="wiki-link" title="Holmium (Ho)">holmium</a>-165 with a very long half-life. Known isotopes of thulium range from <link rel="mw-deduplicated-inline-style" href="mw-data:TemplateStyles:r1123817410" /><span class="chemf nowrap"><sup class="template-chem2-sup">144</sup>Tm</span> to <link rel="mw-deduplicated-inline-style" href="mw-data:TemplateStyles:r1123817410" /><span class="chemf nowrap"><sup class="template-chem2-sup">183</sup>Tm</span>.
</p><p>The primary decay mode before the stable isotope, <link rel="mw-deduplicated-inline-style" href="mw-data:TemplateStyles:r1123817410" /><span class="chemf nowrap"><sup class="template-chem2-sup">169</sup>Tm</span>, is electron capture to <a href="/school1/ptable/element/Er" class="wiki-link" title="Erbium (Er)">erbium</a> isotopes, and the primary mode after is beta emission to <a href="/school1/ptable/element/Yb" class="wiki-link" title="Ytterbium (Yb)">ytterbium</a> isotopes. The longest-lived radioisotopes are thulium-171, which has a half-life of 1.92 years, and thulium-170, which has a half-life of 128.6 days. Most other isotopes have half-lives under 10 minutes.
</p>

--- History ---
<figure class="mw-default-size" typeof="mw:File/Thumb"><img src="/school1/ptable/theme-assets/default/images/inline/9ad4b4844536d6c8a9b249806487b757.jpg" decoding="async" width="190" height="247" style="--mw-file-upright: 0.75" class="mw-file-element mw-file-upright" srcset="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/0/0e/Per_Teodor_Cleve_c1885.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&amp;utm_campaign=parser&amp;utm_content=thumbnail_unscaled 2x" data-file-width="311" data-file-height="404" /><figcaption>Per Teodor Cleve, the scientist who discovered <a href="/school1/ptable/element/Tm" class="wiki-link" title="Thulium (Tm)">thulium</a> as well as <a href="/school1/ptable/element/Ho" class="wiki-link" title="Holmium (Ho)">holmium</a>.</figcaption></figure>
<p><a href="/school1/ptable/element/Tm" class="wiki-link" title="Thulium (Tm)">Thulium</a> was discovered by Swedish chemist Per Teodor Cleve in 1879 by looking for impurities in the oxides of other rare earth elements. This was the same method Carl Gustaf Mosander earlier used to discover some other rare earth elements. Cleve started by removing all of the known contaminants of erbia (<link rel="mw-deduplicated-inline-style" href="mw-data:TemplateStyles:r1123817410" /><span class="chemf nowrap">Er<sub class="template-chem2-sub">2</sub>O<sub class="template-chem2-sub">3</sub></span>). Upon additional processing, he obtained two new substances; one brown and one green. The brown substance was the oxide of the element <a href="/school1/ptable/element/Ho" class="wiki-link" title="Holmium (Ho)">holmium</a> and was named holmia by Cleve, and the green substance was the oxide of an unknown element. Cleve named the oxide thulia and its element <a href="/school1/ptable/element/Tm" class="wiki-link" title="Thulium (Tm)">thulium</a> after Thule, an Ancient Greek place name associated with Scandinavia or Iceland. Thulium's atomic symbol was initially Tu, but later<sup class="noprint Inline-Template" style="white-space:nowrap;">&#91;<i><span title="The time period mentioned near this tag is ambiguous. (April 2022)">when?</span></i>&#93;</sup> changed to Tm to avoid confusion with <a href="/school1/ptable/element/W" class="wiki-link" title="Tungsten (W)">tungsten</a> which was commonly written as Tu around the time of thulium's discovery.
</p><p>Thulium was so rare that none of the early workers had enough of it to purify sufficiently to actually see the green color; they had to be content with spectroscopically observing the strengthening of the two characteristic absorption bands, as <a href="/school1/ptable/element/Er" class="wiki-link" title="Erbium (Er)">erbium</a> was progressively removed. The first researcher to obtain nearly pure thulium was Charles James, a British expatriate working on a large scale at New Hampshire College in Durham, USA. In 1911 he reported his results, having used his discovered method of bromate fractional crystallization to do the purification. He famously needed 15,000 purification operations to establish that the material was homogeneous.
</p><p>High-purity thulium oxide was first offered commercially in the late 1950s, as a result of the adoption of ion-exchange separation technology. Lindsay Chemical Division of American Potash &amp; Chemical Corporation offered it in grades of 99% and 99.9% purity. The price per kilogram oscillated between US$4,600 and $13,300 in the period from 1959 to 1998 for 99.9% purity, and it was the second highest for the lanthanides behind <a href="/school1/ptable/element/Lu" class="wiki-link" title="Lutetium (Lu)">lutetium</a>.
</p>

--- Occurrence ---
<figure class="mw-default-size" typeof="mw:File/Thumb"><img src="/school1/ptable/theme-assets/default/images/inline/87be9e374c40b48575713d434ccffe09.jpg" decoding="async" width="250" height="212" class="mw-file-element" srcset="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/4/41/Monazit_-_Madagaskar.jpg/500px-Monazit_-_Madagaskar.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&amp;utm_campaign=parser&amp;utm_content=thumbnail 2x" data-file-width="1300" data-file-height="1100" /><figcaption><a href="/school1/ptable/element/Tm" class="wiki-link" title="Thulium (Tm)">Thulium</a> is found in the mineral monazite</figcaption></figure>
<p>The element is never found in nature in pure form, but it is found in small quantities in minerals with other rare earths. <a href="/school1/ptable/element/Tm" class="wiki-link" title="Thulium (Tm)">Thulium</a> is often found with minerals containing <a href="/school1/ptable/element/Y" class="wiki-link" title="Yttrium (Y)">yttrium</a> and <a href="/school1/ptable/element/Gd" class="wiki-link" title="Gadolinium (Gd)">gadolinium</a>. In particular, <a href="/school1/ptable/element/Tm" class="wiki-link" title="Thulium (Tm)">thulium</a> occurs in the mineral gadolinite. Like many other lanthanides, thulium also occurs in the minerals monazite, xenotime, and euxenite. Thulium has not been found in prevalence over the other rare earths in any mineral yet. Its abundance in the Earth's crust is 0.5&#160;mg/kg by weight.
</p><p>Thulium makes up approximately 0.5 parts per million of soil, although this value can range from 0.4 to 0.8 parts per million. Thulium makes up 250 parts per quadrillion of seawater. In the Solar System, thulium exists in concentrations of 200 parts per trillion by weight and 1&#160;part per trillion by moles. Thulium ore occurs most commonly in China. Australia, Brazil, Greenland, India, Tanzania, and the United States also have large reserves of thulium. In 2001, the total world reserves of thulium were approximately 100,000 tonnes. Thulium is the least abundant lanthanide on Earth except for the radioactive <a href="/school1/ptable/element/Pm" class="wiki-link" title="Promethium (Pm)">promethium</a>.
</p>

<div class="mw-heading mw-heading3">

--- Biological role ---
<p>Soluble <a href="/school1/ptable/element/Tm" class="wiki-link" title="Thulium (Tm)">thulium</a> salts are mildly toxic, but insoluble <a href="/school1/ptable/element/Tm" class="wiki-link" title="Thulium (Tm)">thulium</a> salts are completely nontoxic. When injected, <a href="/school1/ptable/element/Tm" class="wiki-link" title="Thulium (Tm)">thulium</a> can cause degeneration of the liver and spleen and can also cause hemoglobin concentration to fluctuate. Liver damage from thulium is more prevalent in male mice than female mice. Despite this, thulium has a low level of toxicity.
</p><p>In humans, thulium occurs in the highest amounts in the liver, kidneys, and bones. Humans typically consume several micrograms of thulium per year. The roots of plants do not take up thulium, and the dry matter of vegetables usually contains one part per billion of thulium. Thulium metal has low to moderate toxicity.
</p>
