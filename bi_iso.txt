<link rel="mw-deduplicated-inline-style" href="mw-data:TemplateStyles:r1368532237" /><div role="note" class="hatnote navigation-not-searchable">Main article: Isotopes of <a href="/school1/ptable/element/Bi" class="wiki-link" title="Bismuth (Bi)">bismuth</a></div>
<p>The only primordial isotope of <a href="/school1/ptable/element/Bi" class="wiki-link" title="Bismuth (Bi)">bismuth</a>, <a href="/school1/ptable/element/Bi" class="wiki-link" title="Bismuth (Bi)">bismuth</a>-209, had long been regarded as the heaviest stable nuclide, but was suspected on theoretical grounds to be unstable to alpha decay. This was finally demonstrated in 2003, when researchers at the <a href="/w/index.php?title=Institut_d%27astrophysique_spatiale&amp;action=edit&amp;redlink=1" class="new" title="Institut d&#39;astrophysique spatiale (page does not exist)">Institut d'astrophysique spatiale</a><span class="noprint" style="font-size:85%; font-style: normal;">&#160;&#91;<a href="https://fr.wikipedia.org/wiki/Institut_d%27astrophysique_spatiale" class="extiw" title="fr:Institut d&#39;astrophysique spatiale">fr</a>&#93;</span> in Orsay, France, detected this decay; the best value of the half-life is now <span class="nowrap"><span data-sort-value="7026634307759999999♠"></span>2.01<span style="margin-left:0.25em;margin-right:0.15em;">×</span>10<sup>19</sup>&#160;years</span> (3 Bq/t), over <span class="nowrap"><span data-sort-value="7009100000000000000♠"></span>10<sup>9</sup></span> times longer than the estimated age of the universe. Due to its hugely long half-life, for all known medical and industrial applications, bismuth can be treated as stable. The radioactivity is of academic interest because bismuth is one of a few elements whose radioactivity was suspected and theoretically predicted before being detected in the laboratory. Bismuth has the longest known α-decay half-life, though <a href="/school1/ptable/element/Te" class="wiki-link" title="Tellurium (Te)">tellurium</a>-128 has the longest known by any mode: double beta decay at about <span class="nowrap"><span data-sort-value="7031710046000000000♠"></span>2.25<span style="margin-left:0.25em;margin-right:0.15em;">×</span>10<sup>24</sup>&#160;years</span>.
</p><p>Six isotopes of bismuth with short half-lives (210–215 inclusive, but not 210m) occur in the natural radioactive decay chains of <a href="/school1/ptable/element/Ac" class="wiki-link" title="Actinium (Ac)">actinium</a>, <a href="/school1/ptable/element/Ra" class="wiki-link" title="Radium (Ra)">radium</a>, <a href="/school1/ptable/element/Th" class="wiki-link" title="Thorium (Th)">thorium</a>, and <a href="/school1/ptable/element/Np" class="wiki-link" title="Neptunium (Np)">neptunium</a>, and more have been synthesized. (Though all primordial <sup>237</sup>Np has long since decayed, it is continually regenerated by (n,2n) knockout reactions on natural <sup>238</sup>U.)
</p><p>For medical use, bismuth-213 can be produced, as the parent isotope <a href="/school1/ptable/element/Ac" class="wiki-link" title="Actinium (Ac)">actinium</a>-225, by bombarding <a href="/school1/ptable/element/Ra" class="wiki-link" title="Radium (Ra)">radium</a> with bremsstrahlung photons from a linear particle accelerator. In 1997, an antibody conjugate with bismuth-213 (half-life 45.6 minutes, emits alpha particles) was used to treat leukemia patients, and it has been used in other cancer treatment, for example, in the targeted alpha therapy  experimental program.
</p>
